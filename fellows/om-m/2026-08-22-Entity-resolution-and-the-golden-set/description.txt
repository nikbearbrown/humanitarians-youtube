Entity Resolution and the Golden Set — Week 4 of the Private AI Valuation Agent

Seven companies. A hundred and twenty-eight different names. This week I built the thing that decides which company a filing is actually talking about — and, more importantly, a way to prove whether it works.

What's in this video:
• Why it's hard — Databricks files under 51 different spellings, Space Exploration under 28, Anthropic under 10. Seven companies wearing 128 names between them, hiding inside 3,204,853 distinct issuer names.
• The instrument — a golden set: 322 labelled issuer names covering 7,276 holdings, used to score two systems against identical labels.
• The result — recall went from 0.9792 to 1.0000. But precision barely moved, and on the hardest cases the older, simpler patterns were actually cleaner. The honest claim is recall, not precision.
• One dot — some funds write X.AI CORP, some write XAI CORP. That single character was hiding 85 holdings, and the fund filing it without the dot is the largest holder of that company.
• The part I got wrong — I flagged a holding called OpenAir.com as not one of my companies. I wrote a confident reason and approved my own judgment. Then I read the rows: five holdings, BlackRock and New York Life, all at 687.6869 — which is OpenAI's Series C price to four decimal places. It was OpenAI the whole time.
• What that changes — I withdrew the approval, fixed the matcher, and published the worse number next to the better one. A score you measured after fixing what the test caught isn't a validation. It's just a score.
• And no threshold saves you — a used-car marketplace whose name happens to contain "open" and "AI" scores exactly the same as three entries that really are SpaceX. 0.80, all four. So four cases a run go to a person instead.

Next week: can a language model beat this? And if it can't, I say so.

Two things worth stating plainly. The matcher did not beat the old patterns on precision — it lost on the hardest cases, and this video says so. And 8 of the 322 labels have actually been reviewed by a person; the other 314 are the machine's own work, and one of those 8 already turned out wrong. That's the honest reason to keep checking.

Every number here is queried from the built data before anything is drawn, so a chart cannot drift from the result it describes. No valuation claim is made about any company.
