# FACTCHECK — How Netflix Streams

Every spoken and on-screen claim, its source, and its verdict. Checked 2026-11-10. "The run" means `stream.py` →
`evidence/run.log` / `evidence/results.json` / `evidence/scene.json`. The run makes **real encodes and a simulated
player**: two 10-second, 1920×1080 test clips generated by ffmpeg (a clean test card, ffmpeg's `testsrc2`, and a
grainy zoom, ffmpeg's `mandelbrot` with added noise), each encoded with libx264 at six rungs and scored with VMAF
through ffmpeg's libvmaf; then three simple players (seed 1113) streaming 150 four-second chunks over a simulated
home connection that sags and drops; then a textbook TCP slow-start model of one chunk's download from a far and a
near server. **Nothing here is Netflix's video, Netflix's ladder or Netflix's player.** The clips are synthetic test
material; the players are mine, one of them modelled on the buffer-based idea from a 2014 study run with Netflix.
Facts about Netflix, with URLs, dates and confidence levels: `week-12/_research/facts-netflix.md` (cited below as
§n.n; summarised in SOURCES.md). B01–B06 are HyperFrames compositions with three.js scenes, written by
`build_hf.py` and `hyperframes/scenes/B0x.js` from the run's files.

Confidence: HIGH / MED as graded in the fact sheet; "measured" = a number from the run.

## Arithmetic, checked separately

- **Rung bitrates.** libx264 single pass, `-b:v` at the rung with `-maxrate` 1.1× and `-bufsize` 2×, preset medium.
  The files came out at or under the target: card 231–5,800 kbit/s, grainy 232–5,497 kbit/s (`real_kbps` in
  `results.json`). The grainy clip's 4,300 rung came out at 3,979.
- **VMAF.** Each rung is scaled back up to 1920×1080 (bicubic) and scored against the reference, itself a
  near-lossless libx264 encode (CRF 4) of the generated clip; the score is the mean over all frames.
- **Four megabits.** VMAF 93 lies between the card's 3,000 rung (87.87) and its 4,300 rung (94.60). Interpolating
  in log bitrate: f = (93 − 87.87) ÷ (94.60 − 87.87) = 0.762; exp(ln 3000 + 0.762 × (ln 4300 − ln 3000)) = 3,947
  kbit/s, shown as 3.9 and spoken as "about four".
- **Ten minutes.** 150 chunks × 4 s = 600 s of video.
- **The players.** Always-highest asks for 5,800 every time. Throughput-based takes the highest rung at or below
  0.8 × a running average of past download speeds. Buffer-based spreads the buffer level from 8 s (lowest rung)
  to 38 s (highest) evenly over the six rungs, rounding to the nearest. The buffer is capped at 40 s. A stall is counted when a chunk takes
  longer to arrive than the video left in the buffer; the first chunk's start-up wait is not counted. All three are
  my rules; the buffer-based one follows the idea in §1.6, not its settings (that study used a 90 s reservoir and a
  240 s buffer).
- **The connection.** About 7 Mbit/s with a slow swing and noise; from 150 to 210 s it falls to 28% ("someone
  starts a big download"); from 380 to 400 s it is 0.9 Mbit/s ("a bad patch of Wi-Fi"); never below 0.4. Its
  mean over the 350 samples in `scene.json` is 6.5 Mbit/s (range 0.9 to 9.5).
- **Distance.** One chunk = 5.8 Mbit/s × 4 s = 23.2 Mbit (2.9 MB) over a 50 Mbit/s line on a fresh connection.
  TCP slow start begins at 10 packets of 1,460 bytes and doubles each round trip until the line is full; one more
  round trip for the request. Far (150 ms): 9 rounds + 1 = 10 × 0.15 s = 1.50 s; the window is still growing for
  most of the trip. Near (8 ms): the window fills the line after 2 rounds, 58 full rounds follow, (60 + 1) × 8 ms =
  0.49 s, close to the line's own 23.2 ÷ 50 = 0.46 s. The 150 ms and 8 ms are my choices, not
  measurements.

## Flags, corrected

Raised in the first check on 2026-11-10 and fixed in the film the same day (B04, B06 and B07 re-voiced with Kokoro;
`beat_sheet.json` and `cues.json` regenerated by `author_sheet.py`, then `run_audio.sh`; all 41 cues resolved;
total 128.71 s).

1. **B04 red blocks.** The first build marked a block red when its download outlasted the buffer *or took more than
   8 s*, so slow downloads that did not stall were red too: 6 red blocks on always-the-best (one real stall), 2 each
   on the buffer and speed lanes (none). Now a block is red only where the player emptied its buffer, and the
   build asserts the total against run.log. Checked on `scene.json` (chunks numbered from 0): always-the-best,
   chunk 50, 6.89 s; buffer-based and throughput-based, none. The speed ribbon, first plotted per 2 s of clock
   time on the blocks' spacing, now sits over the chunk the buffer-based player was fetching at that moment, so its
   dips sit over the chunks they slowed. The narration's "looked better" became "streamed at a higher quality", the
   bitrate claim the run supports.
2. **B06, 2022 figures in the present tense.** "That network has about eighteen thousand servers…" became "By
   twenty twenty-two, that network had…", with "Today it serves" for the 2026 membership figure; both cuts now label
   the servers and countries "· 2022".
3. **B07 "more than".** Now "A steady connection matters as much as a fast one", the fact sheet's safe wording; the
   card's "Steady beats fast: use a cable if Wi-Fi drops." became "A steady connection matters too." (phone: "Keep
   it steady").

## Not claimed

- That Netflix uses buffer-based adaptation, or any named algorithm, in its players today (§1, "do not say"): B04
  tests three simple players of my own.
- That Netflix sends 4-second chunks today: the run uses 4 s because the 2013 test setup did (§1.3).
- Any current Netflix ladder: B01's six rungs are mine.
- That my VMAF scores say anything about Netflix's streams: they score my two synthetic clips.
- That Netflix delivers over DASH or HLS (§1.9, "do not say").
- That 1.50 s and 0.49 s are real download times: they are a textbook model with chosen round trips.
- That Netflix invented AV1, or that AV1 is its most-used codec (§2.13, §2.14).
- That Netflix has a billion subscribers: "approaching one billion" is an audience estimate of people (§5.4), and
  the video doesn't use it.
- Any overall Netflix rebuffer rate (§6.5).
