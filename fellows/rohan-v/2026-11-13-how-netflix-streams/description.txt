MANY COPIES, AND A SCORE
Every show is stored many times over, at different qualities: a bitrate ladder. I made two test clips, a clean test
card and a grainy, detailed zoom, and encoded each at six rungs. Netflix built a quality score called VMAF, from 0
to 100, and open-sourced it in 2016. On my test card the lowest rung scored 38 and the top rung 96; the grainy clip
topped out at 75. My test card reached a score of 93 at about 4 Mbit/s; the grainy clip never got there. Since
2015, Netflix has built a ladder for each title.

CHOOSING BEFORE EVERY CHUNK
The show is cut into chunks a few seconds long, and before each one the player picks a rung. I streamed ten
minutes over a simulated connection that sags and drops. Always picking the best quality stalled for 6.9 seconds.
Picking by how full the buffer was never stalled, and streamed at a higher quality (average bitrate) than picking
by speed.

A SERVER NEAR YOU
Netflix's own network, Open Connect, puts its servers inside internet providers and fills them with popular shows
overnight. In my model, one chunk took 1.5 seconds from a far server and 0.5 seconds from one inside your provider.
By 2022, Netflix said, Open Connect had about 18,000 servers in 175 countries. Netflix passed 325
million paid memberships at the end of 2025, and about 30% of its viewing used the AV1 codec as of late 2025.

WHAT TO DO
Netflix suggests about 5 Mbit/s for Full HD and 15 for 4K. A steady connection matters as much as a fast one.

The encodes, scores, player and download numbers above come from my own test clips and simulation, not Netflix's
video, ladder or player. The test clips are synthetic test material made with ffmpeg; the players are simple ones
I wrote. The server, membership, codec and speed figures are Netflix's own, with their dates.
