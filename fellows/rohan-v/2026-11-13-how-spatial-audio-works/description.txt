How do headphones make a sound seem to come from beside you, behind you, or across the room? I built a small
model of a head to see how. Explained from nothing: no technical background needed.

Wear headphones for the clip near the middle (about 1:19): it is one turn of a melody around your head, and it only
works in stereo.

TWO EARS
A sound on your right reaches your right ear first. In my head model, straight out to one side, the gap is about
two thirds of a millisecond, and it shrinks to nothing as the sound moves in front of you. Your head also casts a
shadow: high sounds came out 14 decibels quieter in the far ear in my model, low sounds only about 2. Timing for
low sounds, loudness for high ones, as Lord Rayleigh worked out in 1907.

FRONT OR BEHIND
A sound in front of you and its mirror behind you give your ears the same timing and the same loudness; in my
model, exactly the same. The folds of your outer ears break the tie.

THE FILTERS
Researchers measure all of this with microphones inside a dummy head's ears; MIT's KEMAR measurements cover 710
directions. Those measurements are called head-related transfer functions. Run a sound through the pair for one
direction, and headphones play it from there.

HEAD TRACKING AND YOUR EARS
Turn your head, and an ordinary headphone mix turns with you. Spatial audio uses motion sensors to turn the sound
the other way, so it stays put. Apple added this to AirPods in 2020. Because everyone's ears are different, Apple's
Personalized Spatial Audio scans your ears with the iPhone's camera to build a profile for you.

TO TRY IT
Use headphones with head tracking, set up the personalized profile, and pick music mixed in Dolby Atmos.

The timing, loudness and front/back numbers, and the listening clip, come from my own model of a textbook
spherical head, not from Apple's code; Apple hasn't published how its renderer works. The head, ears and phone on
screen are drawn.
