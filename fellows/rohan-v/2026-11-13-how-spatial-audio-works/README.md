# How Spatial Audio Works — Rohan V.

Audio explainer, week of 2026-11-09 to 2026-11-13 · Humanitarians AI · Lyrical Literacy · GitHub `rohanvijaykumar`

## This week's contribution

**Question.** How do headphones make a sound seem to come from beside you, behind you, or across the room?

**Prediction.** Going in, the working assumption was that the timing and loudness differences between the two ears
would be enough to place any sound, front or back included.

**What I built/tried.** `spatial.py`: the textbook spherical head (8.75 cm radius; Woodworth timing, Brown-Duda
head shadow, a simple outer-ear filter for sounds behind), with a plucked-string melody as the test sound. It
measures the timing gap from 0° to 90°, the far-ear level drop in a low and a high band, a front/back pair, the
head-tracking case, and renders an 8-second binaural clip of the melody circling the head (played in the video,
headphones on). The six body scenes are three.js stages in HyperFrames (`build_hf.py`, `hyperframes/scenes/`).

**Observed result.** The gap grows from 0 ms straight ahead to 0.661 ms straight out to the side. The far ear hears
high sounds (3–6 kHz) 14.0 dB quieter and low sounds (350–700 Hz) only 2.3 dB quieter. A sound at 30° in front and
its mirror at 150° behind gave exactly the same timing (0.266 ms) and spectra (0.00 dB apart), so the prediction
was wrong for front and back; the outer-ear filter separated them by 2.6 dB. Turning the head 40° right needs the
sound rendered at −40° to stay put.

**Next experiment.** Replace the spherical head with a measured set of head-related filters and test with
listeners whether front and back are heard correctly.
