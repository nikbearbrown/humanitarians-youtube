Why Your Track Gets Turned Down — Humanitarians AI

You finish a track, it sounds quieter than everything else in the playlist, so
you push it louder. The platform then turns it back down to the same level as
everyone else. This is about what that actually costs you — and it is not
nothing, because the loudness comes back off and the thing you gave up to get
it does not.

WRITTEN FOR PEOPLE WHO HAVE NEVER OPENED A DAW
No jargon. The two things being measured are called "the tallest point" and
"how loud it actually sounds". The thing you lose is called "the gap".

EVERY NUMBER IN THIS VIDEO WAS MEASURED
The experiment was run before the script was written. Two versions of the same
30-second test signal, measured with ffmpeg's EBU R128 scanner. Their tallest
points are 0.8 dB apart — effectively identical on a peak meter — but one is
8.7 points louder on the loudness scale. To reach a playback target of about
-14, the untouched version needed +1.3 dB and the squeezed one needed -7.4 dB.
Every bit of that hard-won loudness was removed before anyone heard it.

And then the part that does not reverse: the gap between the quietest and
loudest moments fell from 12.0 to 0.7. Applying the same volume change to both
files moved the loudness numbers and left the gap numbers exactly where they
were. Turning a file up or down cannot give back room you already crushed.

A NOTE ON THE NUMBERS
This is a synthetic test signal, chosen so its crest factor and quiet passage
are known exactly rather than guessed. The mechanism generalises; the specific
figures belong to this file. Playback targets differ between platforms and
change over time — the behaviour does not.
