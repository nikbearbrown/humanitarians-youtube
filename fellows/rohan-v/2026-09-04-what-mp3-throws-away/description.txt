What MP3 Throws Away — Humanitarians AI

An MP3 is not a smaller copy of your song. It is a different song — one where
an algorithm decided which parts you would not miss, and then deleted them
permanently. This explains what actually gets removed, why the encoder is
allowed to remove it, and the point at which that removal stops being
acceptable.

EVERY NUMBER IN THIS VIDEO WAS MEASURED
Rather than quoting figures, the experiment was run first: white noise encoded
at 128 and 320 kbps with ffmpeg and libmp3lame, decoded back, and measured band
by band. At 128 kbps, 16 kHz survives almost untouched while 17 kHz is 13.2 dB
down — a cliff, not a fade. At 320 kbps the audio is identical to the source all
the way to 19 kHz. Re-encoding a 128 kbps file ten more times costs the 12 kHz
band another 7 dB, and none of it returns.

WHAT YOU WILL KNOW AFTER WATCHING
What a lossy encoder deletes and why it is entitled to. What psychoacoustic
masking is, in plain language. What the bitrate number actually buys you. Why
editing and re-exporting an MP3 is never a neutral operation. When lossy is
completely fine, and when it is malpractice.

A NOTE ON THE NUMBERS
The exact cliff frequency belongs to this encoder at these settings. Other
encoders and variable-bitrate modes choose different lowpass points. The shape
of the result — a hard cliff whose position tracks the bitrate — is general.
