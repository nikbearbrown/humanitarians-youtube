FINDING THE PITCH
A sung note is a wave that repeats, and how often it repeats is its pitch: the A above middle C repeats 440 times
a second. To find it, the software slides a copy of the wave along itself until the two line up. My detector was
within five cents of the true pitch 93% of the time; five cents is about the smallest change most people can hear.

OFF KEY, AND SNAPPING TO THE NOTE
A semitone, one step on a piano, is 100 cents. My synthetic singer was off by about 29 cents on average: never a
wrong note, just a little sharp or flat. Auto-Tune moves each moment toward the nearest note in the key you choose,
and a setting called retune speed decides how fast. At a gentle 60 milliseconds my singer averaged under seven
cents off and kept the vibrato; at zero the voice sat within five cents of a note 91% of the time. That's the
robotic sound, made famous by Cher's "Believe" in 1998.

SHIFTING WITHOUT CHIPMUNKS
Speed a recording up and its pitch rises, but it gets shorter. Auto-Tune's patent keeps the length by repeating
whole cycles. For a big shift (four semitones in my test), both moved the vowel up by over a quarter. My version
re-spaces single cycles instead, a method called PSOLA, and the vowel moved under one percent.

THEN AND NOW
Auto-Tune came out in 1997, from an engineer who had used similar maths to read seismic data for oil. Today, pitch
correction is a standard studio tool, often too subtle to notice.

TO TRY IT
Set your song's key first, start with a slow retune speed for a natural sound, and turn it to zero for the effect.
