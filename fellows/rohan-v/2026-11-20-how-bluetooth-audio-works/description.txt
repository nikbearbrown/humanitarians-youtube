How does music get from your phone to wireless earbuds, and why does it sometimes lag or cut out? I built a small

TOO MUCH DATA
CD-quality stereo is about 1,400 kilobits a second: more than Bluetooth's audio link can reliably carry. So your
phone squeezes the music first, with a codec. The one every device must support is SBC; on my test track it used
about 330 kilobits a second, under a quarter.

THE RADIO
Classic Bluetooth hops between 79 channels, up to 1,600 times a second, and shares them with Wi-Fi. In my
simulation, a busy Wi-Fi network made 17% of tries fail. Adaptive hopping skips the busy channels: about 1%.

CODECS
Your phone and headphones agree on a codec. SBC is the baseline; Apple's devices prefer AAC; many Android phones
also offer aptX (about 350 kilobits a second) or LDAC (up to 990). More data can sound better, but it's harder to
send without gaps.

THE BUFFER
Lost packets, from Wi-Fi or your own body, get sent again, so the earbuds keep a little music in reserve: a buffer.
In two minutes of my simulation, a 50 millisecond buffer glitched 30 times; 200 milliseconds, 3 times; 300, never.
That reserve is the main reason Bluetooth lags.

LIP SYNC AND WHAT COMES NEXT
Real earbuds are often a couple of hundred milliseconds behind. For videos, the headphones can report that delay,
and the phone holds the picture back to match; a game can't hold back your button presses. Bluetooth's next
generation, LE Audio, brings a new codec called LC3, which the Bluetooth SIG says beats SBC even at half the bit
rate, and Auracast, which lets one phone or TV stream to any number of earbuds nearby.

FOR BETTER SOUND
Keep your phone close, pick a better codec if your phone offers one, and use your earbuds' game mode for games.

The data rates were measured on my own synthetic test track with ffmpeg's codec encoders (ffmpeg's AAC encoder,
not Apple's). The radio, Wi-Fi, body-blocking and buffer numbers come from a simulation with settings I chose, not
