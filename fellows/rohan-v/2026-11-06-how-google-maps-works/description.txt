FOUR SATELLITES AND A CLOCK
Your phone times signals from at least four satellites: three pin down where you are, and the fourth corrects
your phone's clock, because one millisecond off is 300 kilometres. In my simulation, open sky gave about 5
metres and tall buildings about 70. That's the blue circle: how unsure it is.

FINDING A ROUTE
The roads become a graph. In my city of 900 corners, Dijkstra's algorithm checked 826 of them; A-star, which aims
at the destination, checked 547 and found the same route. Google doesn't publish its exact recipe.

PHONES FILL IN THE REST
Since 2009, phones with location on have sent anonymous speeds. In my city, history alone coloured 87% of roads
right but missed a crash; 500 phones turned it red. Arrival times from speed limits alone were 19% off; history
plus live, 3%. Google says its arrival times are accurate for over 97% of trips. Popular times come from people
who opted in: counting a quarter of one cafe's visitors, with privacy noise, still matched the real crowd. With 3
in 100 riders answering "how crowded?", my bus predictions were right for 81% of trips.

WHAT TO DO
When the blue circle is big, trust your eyes. Report crashes and closures. Answer the crowding question.

The GPS, routing, traffic, arrival-time, busyness and crowding numbers above come from my own simulation, not
Google's data; the 97% is Google's own figure. The Google Maps cards, blue dot, pin and route on screen are drawn
to look like the app from my numbers; they are not screenshots.
