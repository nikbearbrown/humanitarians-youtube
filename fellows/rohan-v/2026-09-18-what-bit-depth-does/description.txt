16-bit or 24-bit: What Bit Depth Does — Humanitarians AI

You save a recording and something asks you: 16-bit, or 24-bit? No
explanation, and no default that is obviously right. This is what that number
actually controls, explained from nothing — no audio background needed.

WRITTEN FOR SOMEONE WHO HAS NEVER OPENED AN AUDIO EDITOR
It starts with what sound even is, and what a computer has to do to write it
down. Then a ruler: a ruler marked in centimetres and one marked in
millimetres are the same length, one just lets you be more precise. That is
bit depth, and the catch is that every measurement has to land exactly on a
marking. Anything in between gets pushed to the nearest one.

No jargon. Quantisation is called "pushed to the nearest marking". The noise
floor is called "the hiss the file itself adds". Dynamic range is called "how
quiet a sound can get before that hiss takes over". Sample rate is named once,
so you can tell the two numbers apart, and then set aside.

THE NUMBERS WERE MEASURED, NOT LOOKED UP
The experiment ran before the script was written: a 20-second tone generated
in 64-bit floating point, quantised to each depth by rounding with no dither,
and the leftover error measured against the original.

  8-bit   hiss at -53.1 dB below full volume
  16-bit  hiss at -100.8 dB below full volume

A hundred decibels is roughly the gap between a whisper and a rock concert. So
at 16 bits the file's own hiss is already quieter than the room you are
sitting in. You will never hear it.

WHAT TO DO
Record and edit in 24-bit, because every edit rounds all over again and
turning something up turns its hiss up with it. Export in 16-bit, because your
listener gains nothing from more and the file is a third smaller. Never save
down to 16 and then keep editing — the hiss you added does not come back out.

A NOTE ON THE NUMBERS
The measurement is undithered, which is the simpler case; real 16-bit masters
are usually dithered, trading a slightly higher noise floor for a much better
behaved one. Every measured floor also sits about 3 dB below the textbook
6.02b + 1.76 formula, because that formula assumes a full-scale sine and this
tone peaks at -6 dB. The offset is the same at every depth. The video reports
the measured values.
