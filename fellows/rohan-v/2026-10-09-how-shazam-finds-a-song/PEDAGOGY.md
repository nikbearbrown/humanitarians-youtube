# PEDAGOGY — "How Shazam Finds a Song"

How the reel is built to teach someone who has used Shazam but never been told how it works.

## The register

The standing corrections still apply: *no assumed knowledge* and *the viewer has to learn something*.
So a picture (the spectrogram) comes before any mechanism, there is exactly one equation (a
subtraction), and the reel ends on decisions the viewer can make about the music in their own videos.

## The shape

| Beat | Job | What the viewer can do after it |
|---|---|---|
| B00 ASK | the question: how does an app name a song from five noisy seconds? | name the answer's shape: points, codes, a vote |
| B01 BACKGROUND | the spectrogram, and keeping only the loudest points | read a spectrogram; see why noise doesn't wipe the fingerprint |
| B02 MECHANISM | pairs of points become hashes | explain what a fingerprint actually is |
| B03 MATH | song time minus clip time: a real match agrees | say why chance matches don't fool it |
| B04 MEASURED | my fingerprinter finds Song C in cafe noise, and you hear both | believe it: the test is real, measured and audible |
| B05 NOISE | 180 clips across six noise levels; no wrong answers | judge how much noise it takes |
| B06 THE CATCH | a new key, a faster take or a hum is a different recording | predict when it won't match |
| B07 WHAT TO DO | music under your voice can be found; permission is separate | choose music for a video responsibly |
| B08 OUTRO | sign-off | — |

## Choices worth defending

- **Synthetic songs.** They make every number reproducible, keep copyrighted recordings out of a
  public repo, and let the catch beat re-perform the same song exactly (new key, new speed, hummed).
- **The paper's own scatter plot.** B03 shows the diagonal from the 2003 paper with my own run's
  points, so the idea and the evidence are the same picture.
- **Honest failure.** B05 shows the misses as well as the hits, and the fact that it says "no match"
  rather than guessing is the most useful thing it teaches about the method.
