The Backtest Mirage: why an AI strategy search always finds a "brilliant" winner

Let an AI test enough trading strategies on past data and one of them will always look brilliant, even when there
is nothing to find. In my small experiment on synthetic noise data (pure random daily returns, so no strategy can
have a real edge), a search tries 10,000 simple long/short rules on 5 years of data. The best one scores a Sharpe
ratio of 1.69. On the 2 years it never saw: −0.37. Repeated on 200 fresh noise markets, the best in-sample Sharpe
climbs with the number of rules tried (about 0 for 1 rule, 0.7 for 10, 1.1 for 100, 1.6 for 10,000), while the
winners average about zero on new data. Why: a 5-year Sharpe ratio carries noise of about 1/√5 ≈ 0.45, and the
winner is just the luckiest noise. Bailey, Borwein, López de Prado and Zhu called this backtest overfitting. The
fix: count your trials, hold out data you never touch, and demand more as you test more. Honest limits: synthetic
noise, no trading costs, and real markets may hide small real edges.
