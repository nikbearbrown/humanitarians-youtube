# How Do You Actually Know It Worked?

## The Trust Problem

Here's the uncomfortable starting point. When an agent explains its reasoning — "I checked the revenue figures, cross-referenced them against the filing, and concluded X" — that explanation was generated by the same model that generated the conclusion. It is not a transcript of what actually happened inside the system. It's a plausible-sounding narration, produced after the fact, by something that is very, very good at producing plausible-sounding narrations.

[VISUAL: Split screen. Left: a box labeled "What Actually Happened" — mostly blank, unknown. Right: a box labeled "What the Model Says Happened" — full of confident, well-formatted prose. A dotted line connects them, labeled "assumed to be the same thing"]

A model that hallucinated a number can write a perfectly coherent paragraph about how it derived that number. Fluency and accuracy are two completely different properties, and a language model optimizes hard for the first one. So the real engineering question isn't "does the explanation sound reasonable" — of course it does, that's what these models are built to produce. The question is: can any part of that explanation be checked against something outside the model itself?

## Mechanism 1 — Claim Extraction

The first move is to stop treating the reasoning as one continuous story, and start treating it as a pile of individual, checkable assertions.

[VISUAL: A wall of paragraph text gets run through a shredder-like animation, coming out the other side as individual labeled index cards: "CITATION," "QUANTITATIVE," "HEDGE," "CAUSAL"]

Run the reasoning text through a parser that pulls out four kinds of claims. Citations — anywhere the text points to a specific source, like "[SOURCE: SEC 10-K, sec.gov/...]". Quantitative claims — any dollar figure, percentage, multiple, or basis-point number. Hedge language — words like "approximately," "unclear," "assumed," which flag where the model itself is signaling uncertainty. And causal claims — sentences using words like "because" or "resulting in," which is where a model asserts that one thing caused another, the single easiest kind of claim to get wrong with total confidence.

None of this requires understanding what the text means. It's pattern matching — regular expressions hunting for dollar signs, percent signs, source brackets, and a fixed list of hedge words. That's the point. You're not asking a second AI to judge whether the reasoning sounds good. You're mechanically converting prose into a checklist of specific, falsifiable pieces.

## Mechanism 2 — Verification Against Reality

Extraction gives you a list of claims. It doesn't tell you if any of them are true. That's the next step, and it's the one that actually leaves the model's head entirely.

[VISUAL: One of the citation index cards from before floats up, and an arrow shoots out from it toward a small icon of a government filing document. A checkmark or an X appears depending on what's found]

For every citation claim that points at a real URL, the system actually goes and fetches that source. If it's a government financial filing, it parses the structured data directly — actual reported numbers, not text scraped off a webpage. If it's a generic page, it searches the raw text for numbers. Then it takes every quantitative claim the model made and checks: does a matching number actually show up in that source, within about one percent, to allow for rounding?

Each citation comes back one of three ways. Confirmed — the number is really there. Checked and not found — the source is real and reachable, but the claimed number isn't in it, which is a serious red flag. Or unattainable — the fetch failed, the page was unreachable, so no judgment can be made either way. That third option matters as much as the other two: an honest system has to be able to say "I don't know" instead of quietly defaulting to pass.

[VISUAL: A small percentage counter ticks up on screen: "Verification rate: 6 of 9 citations confirmed — 67%"]

Roll all of that up into a single number — the fraction of citations actually confirmed against real sources — and now you've got something a human reviewer can act on that has nothing to do with how convincing the writing sounds.

## Mechanism 3 — Consistency Probing

Verification catches claims that point at real sources. But plenty of reasoning doesn't cite anything external at all — it's synthesis, judgment, "based on everything above, I conclude X." You can't fetch a source for that. So you use a different trick entirely: ask the same question twice.

[VISUAL: The exact same input goes into the agent box twice, in parallel. Two conclusion outputs come out the other side, and a comparison meter appears between them]

Run the identical query through the full reasoning process a second time, independently. Then compare the two conclusions — how much do the actual words overlap, and, weighted more heavily, how much do the specific numbers overlap. A model that's actually reasoning from real evidence should land in roughly the same place twice. A model that's confabulating a plausible story tends to drift, especially on the specific numbers, because a fabricated number is much harder to fabricate identically twice than a fabricated vibe.

[VISUAL: A results card appears: "HIGH agreement — 0.82" in green, then a second example: "LOW agreement — 0.21, number mismatch flagged" in red, with the two different numbers highlighted side by side]

Score it, classify it — high agreement, medium, low — and raise one specific hard flag whenever a number shows up in one run but not the other at all. That flag alone catches a huge class of problems, because a number that only exists in one of two otherwise-similar runs is one of the strongest available signals that it was invented, not retrieved.

## What This Doesn't Prove

Here's the part that's tempting to skip, and the part that actually matters most. None of these three mechanisms proves the agent reasoned correctly.

[VISUAL: The word "PROOF" appears center screen, then a red line strikes through it, replaced with "EVIDENCE"]

High consistency is weak positive evidence, not a guarantee. Two runs can agree with each other while both being confidently, identically wrong — a model doesn't need real evidence to be consistent, it just needs to draw on the same internalized pattern twice. Verification only checks that a number exists somewhere in the cited source — it can't confirm the agent's causal story about what that number means. And a citation coming back "unattainable" tells you nothing either way.

What these mechanisms are actually good at is the other direction: catching disagreement, catching absence, catching drift. Low consistency is strong negative evidence — it's very hard to accidentally look inconsistent while being right. A citation that comes back "checked and not found" is a real, structural problem, full stop. These tools are built to reliably catch when something is wrong. They were never built to certify that something is right, and treating them as if they could would just relocate the trust problem one layer down instead of solving it.

[VISUAL: A simple two-column card: "Good at catching: fabrication, drift, absent evidence" next to "Cannot prove: correct causal reasoning, sound judgment"]

## The Framework

So here's what actually holds up. One — don't grade the story, grade the claims. Break reasoning into small, checkable pieces before you evaluate any of it. Two — check what can be checked against the outside world, and log honestly when something can't be checked at all, instead of quietly assuming pass. Three — when nothing external exists to check against, make the system disagree with itself on purpose, because genuine drift under repetition is one of the few signals a fabrication can't easily fake.

[VISUAL: End card. Three icons stack vertically: a shredder (extraction), a magnifying glass over a filing (verification), a mirror (consistency) — each with a small green check underneath, and a footnote reads: "None of these prove truth. All three narrow what's plausible."]

An agent telling you it succeeded was never good enough. Now you know what "actually checking" looks like — and just as importantly, what it still can't tell you. That gap is exactly where the next problem lives: what happens when you've got more than one agent, and they don't agree with each other at all.

## Key Takeaways

1. **A reasoning trace is an artifact, not a transcript.** The explanation a model gives for its own conclusion was generated by the same system that generated the conclusion — fluency is not evidence of an accurate process. (The core lesson behind ADR-06: "the log is evidence of output, not evidence of process.")
2. **Decompose before you judge.** Turning free-text reasoning into typed, structured claims (citation / quantitative / hedge / causal) via mechanical pattern-matching — not a second AI's opinion — is what makes reasoning auditable at all.
3. **Verification means leaving the model's head entirely.** A citation is only meaningful once it's checked against an independently fetched source, with an honest third outcome ("unattainable") for when no judgment can be made — silently defaulting to pass is worse than not checking.
4. **Consistency is a probe, not a proof.** Running the same query twice and measuring drift (especially in specific numbers, which are harder to fabricate identically than general phrasing) catches confabulation without needing any external ground truth — but agreement between two runs is still not the same as correctness.
5. **Know what your checks can't tell you.** Every verification mechanism has a direction it's strong in (catching fabrication, absence, drift) and a claim it can never make (certifying that the underlying reasoning was actually sound). Confusing "we checked" with "it's correct" just moves the trust problem one layer down instead of closing it.
