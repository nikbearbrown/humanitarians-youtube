# FACTCHECK — chatbot-brand-check

Source: the user-supplied article "Have You Asked the Chatbot About Your Brand
Lately?" (pasted in the build request) and its six references. Every figure
below is stated in that article with the attribution shown; none was added or
changed. **Most sources are companies that sell marketing, PR, or
brand-monitoring services, so the statistics are industry-reported, not
independent research** — said in narration (B10), on every stat card and on the
sources card. I did not re-verify the figures against the original reports; they
are reported as the article reports them, and labeled that way.

1. **Opening scene** (B01) — someone hears about a small bakery from a friend,
   skips the map, asks a chatbot "What's good at Maple Street Bakery, and when is
   it open?" and decides whether to visit from the answer. **An illustration, not
   a real case** — the article says so; narration ("This is an illustration, not
   a real case.") and an on-screen label say so. "Maple Street Bakery" is the
   article's own invented example.

2. **900 million weekly ChatGPT users, Feb 2026** (B02) — article, cited to
   BrandMentions (2026). Per the article's source note, the OpenAI figure comes
   secondhand through BrandMentions; the card says "OpenAI figure, via
   BrandMentions, 2026".

3. **49% of U.S. adults have used an AI chatbot; 42% use one to look up
   information** (B02) — Pew Research Center, reported secondhand through
   BrandMentions (2026); the card says "Pew Research Center, via BrandMentions".
   Narration states the 49%; the 42% is on the card only.

4. **UK survey of 2,564 consumers, April 2026: 78% use AI chat tools; 52% of those
   users said the tools help them find brands they would not have discovered
   otherwise** (B02) — CloudNine PR (2026) press release. Narration says "in one
   U.K. survey, fifty-two percent of users said these tools help them find
   brands…"; the 78% and the sample size are on the card.

5. **1,704 brands graded 32,556 facts AI tools stated about them; about three in
   four brands found at least one false fact; small organizations saw false facts
   about 50% more often than large firms; pricing, funding and which tools a
   product works with were most often wrong** (B03, B04) — Searchable (2026,
   July 30), "The AI brand misinformation report". Per the article's source note,
   **the Searchable results come from brands grading the facts themselves, so
   they are not a random sample**: said in B10 narration ("one study was graded
   by the brands themselves") and on the card, the B03 source chip and the
   sources card. Narration rounds "1,704" to "seventeen hundred" and "32,556" to
   "thirty-two thousand"; the exact numbers are on screen.

6. **About 4% to 7% of brand claims were wrong (counting claims, not brands)**
   (B04) — Sanbi (2026); per the article's source note this comes through Sanbi
   from a study by Profound (card: "Sanbi, 2026 (a study by Profound)"). The
   "two numbers do not conflict" explanation (one asks "did any mistake show up?",
   the other "how many statements were wrong?") is the article's own. The row of
   20 dots on the card is labeled "illustrative: 1 wrong claim in 20" (5% is
   inside the 4%–7% range); it is a visual aid, not a reported figure.

7. **Most errors trace back to real pages (old pricing page, outdated review, old
   comparison article); in more than half of the brands studied the brand's own
   pages were among the sources behind the mistake** (B05) — Sanbi (2026). The
   wording "an old comparison article" shortens the article's "a comparison
   article written before a product changed".

8. **Some chatbots answer from memory that stops at a training date, so a new
   address or price may be missing** (B05) — SubscribePR (2026).

9. **More than 25 million AI-cited links: coverage written by others (news,
   reviews) made up most of the citations** (B06) — BrandMentions (2026). The
   card gives no ratio for "most" because the article gives none.

10. **About 53% of ChatGPT's citations came from managed listings like business
    profiles, about 40% from brand websites** (B06) — Yext (2026). The card says
    "about" on both numbers.

11. **Survey of 3,848 people: 95% took at least one extra step (such as visiting
    the website, reading reviews or checking a profile) before acting on an AI
    recommendation** (B07) — Yext (2026). Narration says "thirty-eight hundred
    people" (rounded from 3,848; exact number on the card) and omits the
    examples; the card lists them.

12. **Only 4% of AI users in the UK survey would buy from an unfamiliar brand right
    away** (B07) — CloudNine PR (2026).

13. **The five-minute check** (B08) — the five steps are the article's own, in
    its order: ask two or three chatbots (Sanbi, 2026: the same mistake rarely
    shows up everywhere); ask like a customer would (what is it, what does it
    cost, where and when, is it any good, alternatives); note what is right, wrong
    and missing and any sources named; fix it at the source and make profiles and
    review sites match each other and the website (Yext, 2026); check again in a
    few weeks. Nothing is added to the steps.

14. **Closing** (B09) — "not about gaming a chatbot… accurate, current information
    easy to find… small teams, including nonprofits and local organizations" is the
    article's bottom line. The closing scene (a small team looking at an accurate
    profile with hours, address and prices ticked) is a generated illustration and
    is labeled as one.
