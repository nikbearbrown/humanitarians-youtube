# FACTCHECK — tutor-they-never-had

Every claim traces to the user-supplied article, "The Tutor They Never Had:
What AI Tutoring Could Mean for Kids Who Have Never Had Extra Help," which
cites six sources. Per DOUBLE-CHECK LAW, single-source and early-stage
findings are narrated with soft attribution ("in one study," "one summary
reports," "seemed to") and flagged on screen, never stated as settled fact.

1. **A kid stuck on math with no one to ask falls behind and may decide they
   are "bad at math"** (B01) — the article's own framing/opening question, not
   a statistic; no source needed. The two-path diagram is an illustration.

2. **Private tutoring can cost $2,500+ per student; ~16 million low-income
   children have been waiting for spots in good afterschool programs** (B02)
   — per the article, citing *Improving Student Learning* (arXiv, 2023). Shown
   as a cost card and a 16-dot grid (each dot = 1 million; the dot count is
   the figure itself, nothing extrapolated).

3. **AI tutors could be patient, always available, never embarrassed by a
   repeated question** (B03) — presented as "the idea," a description of the
   concept, not a result. The chat on screen is an illustrative mock-up, not
   a real transcript.

4. **Hybrid human-tutor + AI-software study in low-income middle schools: the
   furthest-behind students seemed to gain most; it worked best when tutors
   weren't stretched across too many students** (B04) — per the article,
   citing *Improving Student Learning* (2023), one quasi-experimental study.
   Narrated as "in one study" and "seemed to," ending with "early results,
   not a guarantee."

5. **~$700 per student per year vs. ~$3,500–$4,300 for traditional intensive
   tutoring** (B04) — per the article, citing Edcafe AI (2026), an
   AI-education vendor blog summarizing the research. Narrated with the
   user's required hedge, "One summary reports...," followed by "if it holds
   up"; the bars carry an on-screen "one summary reports" flag. The $2,500+
   bar is the private-tutoring figure from claim 2, a different measure from
   "traditional intensive tutoring," and is labeled separately.

6. **Ghana: a math tutor delivered through WhatsApp helped students make
   strong progress over eight months, partly because it ran on phones people
   already had and partly because few of those students had had a tutor**
   (B05) — per the article, citing NHSJS (2025), a literature review. The
   map is a deliberately simplified hand-drawn outline, labeled "simplified
   map"; Ghana's marker is approximate.

7. **~9 million US students lack reliable home internet** (B06) — per the
   article, citing Valere (2026).

8. **In one online tutoring program many students still did not take part
   after receiving free laptops and Wi-Fi** (B06) — per the article, citing
   eSchool News (2024). "A device is not the same as time, a quiet space, and
   a reason to keep going" is the article's own interpretation.

9. **A recent university study with 2,000+ college students found slightly
   lower grades (and less use of other course materials) with an AI tutor;
   adults; researchers noted limits** (B07) — per the article, citing EdTech
   Innovation Hub (2026). The video says "a recent university study," as the
   article does, and stresses both caveats.

10. **Closing view: most promising for kids who would otherwise have no
    tutor, especially with a real person nearby; the question is reach and
    who will be beside the child** (B08) — the article author's own
    conclusion, presented as such ("here's where I've landed"), not as a
    finding.

## What's NOT claimed

No claim that AI tutoring "works" in general, that the cost figures are
guaranteed, or that AI tutors should replace people. No named product is
endorsed. No outcomes are stated beyond what the article states.

The user asked for a personal, warm, neutral, simple tone that presents the
hopeful findings and the cautions fairly. This is a deliberate departure from
this chassis's default Teardown register, made per explicit instruction.
