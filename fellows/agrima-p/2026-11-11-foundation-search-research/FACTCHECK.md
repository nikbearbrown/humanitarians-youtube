# FACTCHECK — foundation-search-research

Source: the user-supplied article "Your Foundation Search Is Doing Market Research"
(pasted in the build request) and its two references: Arbelle (2026, January 19),
*Beauty consumer trends 2026: How to win the modern beauty consumer*, and Mani &
Narayanan (2026, May 19), *Beauty and the (AI) beast: The makeover of
personalisation, simplicity, and trust*, PwC Malaysia. Every claim below is stated
in that article with the attribution shown; none was added or changed. **Arbelle
sells virtual try-on and shade-finder technology, so its customer results are
company-reported, not independent research; PwC's report focuses on the Asia
Pacific market** — said in narration (B04, B09), on the B04 cards and on the
sources card. I did not re-verify the figures against the original reports; they
are reported as the article reports them, and labeled that way.

1. **The late-night try-on** (B01) — 11 p.m., a shopper on a makeup brand's site
   taps "try on", tests three foundation shades (too pale, too orange, almost
   right), is distracted by a text, closes the tab and buys nothing; a small clue
   arrives on the brand's side. **An illustration, not a real case** — the article
   says so; narration ("This is an illustration, not a real case.") and an on-screen
   label say so. The face is an abstract outline with swatches, no photo.

2. **A try-on is a quiet conversation** (B02) — Arbelle (2026): every interaction
   creates insight; brands can see which products are tried most, where customers
   hesitate, and how personalization affects their confidence to buy; it shows what
   people do, not just what they say, and does not depend on a written review. The
   three insight bars on the brand card are decorative, not data. Narration shortens
   "the article's" "how they engage with a product page" part; the rest is as written.

3. **Three things the data can guide** (B03) — Arbelle (2026): product development
   (what to make), merchandising (what to stock and show), marketing (what to say).

4. **A data point that improves the model and the product; small local trends**
   (B03) — PwC Malaysia (Mani & Narayanan, 2026): every interaction with AI-powered
   beauty labs becomes a data point that improves both the model and the product;
   brands can spot small local trends such as "glass skin" in Korea and "no-makeup"
   in Japan. The article adds that brands can use these to tailor recommendations,
   content and R&D; the narration and card keep only the first two points.

5. **cosnova** (B04) — Arbelle (2026) reports that cosmetics company cosnova added AI
   shade matching: over 90% consumer satisfaction, an increase of more than 20% in
   add-to-cart rates, and insights from tens of thousands of shopper sessions.
   **Company-reported** — the card is badged COMPANY-REPORTED, narration says
   "The company says it saw…" and "these numbers are company-reported, not
   independent research", and the footer repeats that Arbelle sells this technology.
   The 90% meter and 30-dot grid are visual aids, not extra figures.

6. **How a gap becomes visible** (B05) — the deep-shade pattern (many shoppers keep
   trying the deepest foundation shades and leave without buying; maybe the range has
   no close match; without try-on data the brand might never see it) is the author's
   own example, **labeled "EXAMPLE -- not a real case"** on screen and "not a real
   case" in narration. The nine-swatch bar, the ticks and crosses are illustrative.
   Arbelle (2026): shoppers want to see themselves reflected in shade ranges and
   brands should review ranges against real customer needs. PwC (2026): some beauty
   algorithms were built on narrow datasets, which can lead to shade matchers that
   miss mid-to-deep tones; test regularly for performance across skin tones; keep
   humans in the loop (beauty advisors, dermatologists checking results). Narration
   says narrow data "can make shade matchers miss mid-to-deep tones" (the article's
   "some … can lead to"). "The data raises the question. A person decides." is the
   article's own line.

7. **Back to the shelf** (B06) — PwC (2026): recommendation data can inform which
   products to keep, so confusing long-tail items can be trimmed and the best products
   get more attention. The shopper takeaway (fewer near-duplicate shades, an easier
   time finding yours) is the article's own "could mean". Arbelle (2026): tools like
   these can reduce returns, increase basket size, and cut sampling waste and
   over-ordering. The shelf of ten near-duplicate bottles is an illustration.

8. **The part that needs care** (B07) — PwC (2026): personalized beauty
   recommendations often rely on sensitive information such as face scans, skin
   condition and behavior patterns; in Asia Pacific about seven in ten people say
   they are concerned about how companies collect their information; advice: collect
   only what's necessary, explain what it's used for, process sensitive data on the
   device when possible, give clear ways to opt out. The ten dots (7 filled) are a
   visual aid for "about 7 in 10". Narration shortens "what it's used for" to "what
   it's for". PwC's Asia Pacific focus is stated on the B09 card.

9. **The closing idea** (B08) — the author's own conclusion: you may have just told a
   brand something useful, not through a survey or a review, just by looking for the
   right match; used well, good for everyone (better matches, a wider range that
   includes shoppers, a clearer idea of what to make next). The shopper and the
   product team are abstract figures, no faces.

10. **Sources note** (B09) — two sources as plain text (no logos); on-screen notes
    that Arbelle sells try-on and shade-finder technology (results company-reported),
    that PwC is a consulting firm whose report focuses on the Asia Pacific market, and
    that the late-night scene and the deep-shade example are illustrations — all from
    the article's own "A note on sources".
