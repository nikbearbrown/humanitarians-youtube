| 1.1 | van Noorden & Moelants (1999) model perceived-tempo distributions as a resonance with natural frequency **2 Hz** (500 ms period), damping constant 1.12, peaking near **120 BPM** | search-summary + citing-source | [Resonance in the Perception of Musical Pulse (ResearchGate)](https://www.researchgate.net/publication/278439304_Resonance_in_the_Perception_of_Musical_Pulse); [Deviations from the resonance theory of tempo induction](https://www.researchgate.net/publication/245915022_Deviations_from_the_resonance_theory_of_tempo_induction) | ✅ attribution confirmed across multiple independent citing works; 1999 primary not yet read |

| 1.3 | **Individual spontaneous motor tempo varies far more than "120" suggests.** Hine et al. (2022), N = 23 (ages 20–25): mean preferred tapping tempo **104.6 BPM** (SD 25.3, range **54–170**) | primary read | [Hine, Abe, Kinzuka, Shehata, Hatano, Matsui & Nakauchi (2022), *Front. **Consequence for the script.** 1.2 and 1.3 do not agree, and the script must not hide
that. 120 BPM is where the *population-level resonance model peaks* — it is not the
average person's tap, and it is certainly not every person's tap. Any beat that says "your
preferred tempo is 120" is false. The defensible sentence is about the peak of a
distribution with a wide spread.

tuned *resonance* in head-acceleration spectra versus the metabolic cost of sustaining that
cadence. 2 Hz is where the body rings, and it is also about 5 METs of effort. Collapsing
these into "people walk at 120 BPM all day" would be wrong. Worth a beat.

2.6 matters for honesty: the resonance is measured, the vestibular explanation for *why*
is the authors' speculation. Do not narrate the mechanism as established.

## Leg 3 — speech rhythm (where the story turns)

| # | Claim | Level | Source | Status |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| 3.1 | **Corrected.** The 2 Hz stress rate is stated **for English specifically**, not for all languages. Verbatim: "the rhythm of stress foot **in English** is centered at half this speed, nominally around **2 Hz**" (citing Ding et al. 2017; Greenberg 1999; Greenberg et al. 1996; Tilsen & Arvaniti 2013; Tilsen & Johnson 2008) | **primary read** | [He, Buder & Bidelman (2024), *Brain and Language* 256:105463 (PMC11422791)](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC11422791/) | ✅ |

Aubanel, Davis & Kim (2016): "across languages the syllable rate is between **5 and 8 Hz**" (citing Pellegrino et al. 2011) | **primary read (both)** | [He et al. 2024](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC11422791/); [Aubanel, Davis & Kim (2016), *Front. | 3.3 | English stressed syllables occur at a rate **below 4 Hz** (citing Greenberg et al. 2003) | **primary read** | [Aubanel, Davis & Kim (2016), PMC5006149](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC5006149/) | ✅ — independent corroboration of 3.1 |

| 3.7 | ~~Speech intelligibility degrades sharply when low-pass filtered below 2 Hz, and improves only marginally above 8 Hz~~ | — | — | ❌ **dropped.** A search summary attributed this to Aubanel et al. **Consequence for the script.** 3.4 is the spine. And 3.1 sharpens it: the 2 Hz stress
figure was *always* an English-language fact in the source literature — the universality
was imported by the retelling, not by the research. So the video's correction is not
"a study contradicts the story"; it is "the story overstated what its own sources said."

N = 34, single-channel EEG, and the authors' own hedges (3.6) mean this is one study, not a
settled result. The script says so out loud.

2 Hz is **step** frequency, not stride (2.4).
