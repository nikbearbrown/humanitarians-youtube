## 1. What the topic already has

The folder is a 10-beat template conversion. Strip the scaffolding (greeting, "Recap with Claude",
"Why this matters", program blurb, "Your turn" prompt, outro, and a filler beat — "The idea should
feel reductive. The digressions earn their place.") and two real ideas remain:

1. **Space shapes music.** Bach wrote for rooms with particular echo; Mozart played palace chambers,
   not symphony halls. (Byrne, ch. 1, "Creation in Reverse", expanded from his 2010 TED talk.)
2. **Technology shapes music.** Edison's cylinders were fragile; bands re-performed the same song for
   each batch. Recording changed what music *becomes*, not only how it is heard.

The template's own ASK beat gestures at the research question this film picks up: *how would a
room's reverberation push a performer toward slower or sparser playing?* `SOURCES.md` records no
statistics. Everything below is new research layered on that base.

## 2. What the research adds

| Layer | Finding | Source (to FACTCHECK against the primary) |
|---|---|---|
| Byrne's claim | Music is made, consciously or not, to fit the place it will be heard: chant for long-decay stone, layered drumming for open air, the dry, absorbent CBGB for detailed rock. He says Carnegie Hall sounds *worse* for that music. | Byrne, *How Music Works*, ch. 1; NPR interview |
| Old advice | Quantz (1752) told flautists to slow down in reverberant rooms so notes don't blur. | Quantz, *Versuch*, via Schärer Kalkandjiev & Weinzierl |
| **The test** | Cellist Jean-Guihen Queyras played all six Bach cello suites in **seven European halls**. He slowed down in the most reverberant halls, as Quantz advised, **and also in the driest ones**: a U-shape. Early decay time explained **74.36%** of his tempo variance. His explanation: in wet rooms he slows to avoid mud and lengthens pauses; in dead rooms he stretches notes to make up for the missing decay. | Schärer Kalkandjiev & Weinzierl, Vienna Talk 2015, pp. 215–219; Psychomusicology 25(3), 2015, DOI 10.1037/pmu0000065 |
| The lab | **12 musicians, 14 simulated halls.** Room acoustics explained only **8.73%** of tempo, even with each musician modelled separately. Strategies were "very individual": slow pieces got longer pauses, fast pieces got shorter, cleaner articulation. Earlier studies (Cleveland Orchestra; a MIDI grand piano in a variable room) found no simple tempo–reverb line at all. | same paper, §§1, 4 |
| Outside the hall | Great tits in noisy parts of Leiden sing with a **higher minimum frequency**, lifting the song above low traffic rumble, within a single bird's repertoire. | Slabbekoorn & Peet, *Nature* 424:267 (2003) |
| The format as a room | Stravinsky's *Serenade in A* (1925): four movements, each written to fit one side of a 10-inch 78 for his Brunswick contract. | Boosey & Hawkes work note; Stravinsky, *Autobiography* |
| The phonograph's ear | Continuous violin vibrato spread with early recording (Katz's "phonograph effect"). | Katz, *Capturing Sound* (2004/2010), ch. "Aesthetics out of Exigency" |
| The newest room | Top-10 US singles, 1986–2015 (303 songs): the instrumental intro fell from about 20 seconds to about 5. | Léveillé Gauvin, *Musicae Scientiae* (2018) |

## 3. The angle

> **A room doesn't write music. It asks a question, and every player hears it and answers
> differently.**

Byrne is right that context shapes music; this film takes that as given. What the measurements add
is *how*. The shaping goes through an ear. The cellist heard each hall and changed his playing,
and his answer in the deadest hall surprised the researchers as much as the wettest. Hand the
same question to twelve players and you get twelve answers, so the room alone predicts very little.
The bird, Stravinsky's contract and the streaming skip button work the same way. Each is a context
that someone *heard* and answered.

**The turn that carries the film:** "He slowed down in the most echoing hall. Quantz would have
nodded. Then he slowed down in the driest hall too." That U-shape breaks the simple story
(echo blurs notes, so play slower) and opens the real one (the player is filling what the room
takes away *and* what it fails to give).

**The payoff, unique to this film:** the cold-open sentence comes back at the end, spoken twice:
once in the most reverberant room and once in the driest. Both deliveries are slower than normal,
for opposite reasons, so the viewer *hears* the U-shape instead of being told it. The film ends on
that sound, with no task, no question and no rubric.

## 4. Structure: *the voice changes rooms*

The organising device is the narration's own sound. Each act is heard **inside** the acoustic it
describes: the Kokoro voice convolved with a measured impulse response. The narration then adapts
on screen and in the ear, the way the cellist did:

1. **Cold open, in a cathedral.** One sentence spoken at normal pace drowns in its own decay. Spoken
   again, slower, it survives. Quantz's 1752 advice, demonstrated in our own medium.
2. **The claim.** Byrne: the place writes the song (CBGB vs Carnegie Hall, chant vs drumming).
3. **Seven halls.** Queyras and the Bach suites. The reverb tail on screen lengthens hall by hall
   as his tempo bends.
4. **The driest hall.** The U-turn. Our voice in a near-anechoic room sounds clipped and thin; we
   stretch it, as he did.
5. **Twelve players, fourteen rooms.** The same question, different answers. Show the two
   strategies (longer pauses vs shorter notes), not a scatter of averages.
6. **Outdoors.** The great tits of Leiden: a context made of noise, not echo, answered in pitch.
7. **The machine as a room.** The 78 side (Stravinsky's stopwatch), the phonograph and vibrato,
   the skip button and the 5-second intro.
8. **Two rooms, one sentence.** The cold-open line returns, spoken in the wettest room and in the
   driest. Both are slowed, for opposite reasons. Byrne's "creation in reverse" lands as something
   heard, not concluded.

Beat count, runtime and title are decided at the beat-sheet stage, not here.

### Why this structure belongs only to this topic

The subject is sound in space, so the film's form *is* sound in space. No previous film could use
it: every earlier device was visual or logical. Checked against the Weeks 15–25 structural audit:

| Already used (W15–W25) | This film |
|---|---|
| Numbered N-question framework (~8 films) | No framework; nothing numbered for the viewer to apply |
| Rooms of a house with lights on/off (W24) | Real acoustics *heard*, not metaphorical chapters; no per-room verdicts |
| Instrument/dial with readings (W20) | No readings taken; the narration itself is transformed |
| Self-run experiment, null result (W17), candidate answers tried in sequence (W25) | We run no experiment. One demonstration (the cathedral line), then published research |
| Trial with witnesses (W22), genealogy/timeline (W23) | The order is acoustic (wet → dry → noise → machine → your ear), not chronological or adversarial |
| "Your turn" task, comments question, quiz, rubric, aphorism | **Ending is a sonic callback:** the opening sentence, re-heard in two opposite rooms |

## 7. Known risks before scripting

- **Byrne quotes:** the book text must be checked first-hand. Online summaries paraphrase; one
  misattributes "great acoustics" to CBGB, where Byrne's point is *little reverberation*.
- **The 74.36% figure** is one cellist, one hall set. Say exactly that.
- **CD/Beethoven's Ninth** is a contested anecdote with conflicting versions. Left out.
- **Neighbour boundary:** any beat that drifts toward headphones, phones, streaming loudness or
  encoding is cut at Gate P (see §6).
- **Music rights:** no commercial recordings. Use our own narration plus impulse responses with a
  licence that allows monetised use (to be verified per file), and synthesised or public-domain
  material only.
- **Scholarly access:** the LibKey connector isn't available in this session. Sources were reached
  through open repositories (TU Berlin DepositOnce, Vienna Talk proceedings, PubMed); full texts of
  Katz and Léveillé Gauvin still need checking.
