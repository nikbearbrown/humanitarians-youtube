How a Room Changes Music: One Cellist, Seven Concert Halls

David Byrne, the singer from Talking Heads, wrote a whole book arguing that music is shaped by the
room it's played in. I wanted to know how that shaping actually happens, so I went looking for
people who measured it.

A renowned cellist played all six of Bach's cello suites in seven European concert halls while two
researchers in Berlin measured each hall's echo and his tempo. In the most echoing halls he slowed
down, as musicians have advised since at least 1752. Then the surprise: in the driest halls, he
slowed down too. Same answer, opposite questions. And when twelve more musicians faced fourteen
simulated halls, each one answered differently. The room doesn't write the music. It asks a
question, and every player answers it their own way. Halfway through, you get to try it in your
own room.

What this video claims, and what it does not:

- 74% is one cellist in seven halls: one measure of each hall's echo accounted for about 74% of the
  variation in his tempo. It is a strong pattern for one player, not a law for everyone.
- Why he slowed down in the echoing halls is his own account (the researchers interviewed him). Why
  he slowed down in the dry halls is the researchers' explanation (holding notes longer to make up for
  missing echo), and the video labels it that way.
- Across twelve musicians in fourteen simulated halls, the hall's echo explained under 9% of tempo,
  and the paper calls their strategies "very individual". The "decide or ask" test in the video is my
  own reading of those two results, labelled "our test".
- The tempo curve on screen is a schematic, marked "shape only". The paper reports a fitted curve,
  not per-hall values, and no data points are drawn.
- The cathedral is simulated: our own impulse response (a measured decay of 4.7 seconds), not a
  recording of any real building. The demonstration shows the echo dying away between phrases; it
  makes no claim about how much of the sentence a listener understands.
- The clap test is a listening rule of thumb, not a measurement.
- A shorter intro doesn't make a hit: the same researcher's second study, comparing hits with less
  popular songs by the same artists, did not support that.
