## The shape: THE FILM RE-COLORS ITSELF

The frame is its own demonstration. Its palette follows what the soundtrack feels like: a quick major passage warms and lightens it, a slow minor passage cools and darkens it. A two-scale **strip** (HAPPY–SAD across, STRONG–WEAK up) sits at the lower left **from the first frame**, and its marker moves with each passage. The viewer matches before being told (B01, B10), and the opening pair returns swapped (B12).

**The reusable method (on screen from frame one, explained in B04, used in B05 and B10):**

> Don't compare the color to the sound. Rate the sound on happy–sad and strong–weak, rate each color on the same two scales, and see whether they agree.

*The two scales are the paper's two emotion dimensions, from its multidimensional scaling of both the music and the colors.*

### The bridge

**B03 · THE STUDY · ~38 s** (tone: curious, concrete)
> In twenty thirteen, Stephen Palmer and colleagues at Berkeley played eighteen pieces of orchestral music by Bach, Mozart and Brahms, fifty seconds each. Some were slow, some medium, some fast, in major and minor. About ninety seven people, in the United States and in Mexico, listened. For each piece, they picked the five colors that fit best from a set of thirty seven, and the five that fit worst. In both countries, quick, major music drew lighter, more saturated, yellower colors. Slow, minor music drew darker, greyer, bluer ones.

*Visual:* a 3 × 2 grid: columns SLOW / MEDIUM / FAST, rows MAJOR / MINOR; each cell holds three small tune tiles (B, M, B-r initials) = 18. Beside it, a 37-swatch array (8 hues × 4 lightness-saturation levels, plus greys, white, black), five swatches ringed green and five ringed red for one tune. The FAST-MAJOR and SLOW-MINOR corners are tinted warm and cool. **Illustration of the design and the direction of the finding; the paper's own figures are not reproduced.**
*Source on screen:* "Palmer, Schloss, Xu & Prado-León, *PNAS* 110(22), 2013. UC Berkeley and Univ. of Guadalajara. 48 US + 49 Mexican listeners."

**B04 · THE GO-BETWEEN · ~38 s** (tone: leaning in; **method explained, before further examples**)
> Here's what they think is underneath. People also rated every piece, and every color, on feelings: happy, sad, strong, weak and more. Rate the music, rate the colors it drew, and the two line up. Point nine seven for happy to sad. Point nine six for strong to weak. Across all four word pairs, point eight nine to point nine nine. So the idea is that color doesn't hear the music. Both carry the same feeling. One honest limit: these are correlations. The authors say so, and they say a direct link isn't ruled out.

*Visual:* the strip enlarges; a music dot and a color dot land on the same spot; the dotted music→color line is struck through and replaced by two lines running through the strip. Four small rows list the four pairs with their values (happy/sad .97, lively/dreary .99, strong/weak .96, angry/calm .89), US figures. A card line "correlation, not proof of mechanism" stays up with the rows. Held 3 s.
*Source on screen:* "Emotion ratings of the 18 pieces vs. of the colors picked for them, US participants (Palmer et al. 2013). Mexico: .97, .96, .96, .93."

**B05 · WORKED EXAMPLE · ~34 s** (tone: steady, step by step)
> Run it with the paper's own two examples. Bach's fast dance in F major: rate it happy, strong. Which colors sit in that corner? The paper says saturated yellow and light yellow. Now Brahms's slow Adagio in C minor: sad, weak. Black, dark grey, dark cyan. The step in the middle is the rating. The sound never has to touch the color.

*Visual:* the strip's marker lands HAPPY/STRONG, then a row of swatches slides past, each rated on the same strip as it passes; yellow and light yellow land in the same corner and the sky fills in yellow. Then the marker goes SAD/WEAK; black, dark grey, dark cyan land; the sky cools. The three-step method card appears: "1 rate the sound · 2 rate the color · 3 do they agree?"
*Source on screen:* "The paper's own example of its idea (Palmer et al. 2013)."

### The edges

**B06 · WHY A COLOR HAS A CATEGORY · ~24 s** (tone: curious)
> Why would color be ready to carry feeling at all? In an essay we read, babies sort colors into categories by about six months, and name them by about eighteen. So the sorting comes before the words. A color can mean something to you before you have a word for it.

*Visual:* a timeline with two ticks (6 mo, 18 mo) over a wheel that goes from grey to a handful of color blobs; "category" at 6 months, "name" at 18.
*Source on screen:* "From an essay we read."

**B07 · WHERE THE MATCH STOPS · ~38 s** (tone: honest, a little cautious)
> How far does the match travel? Between the United States and Mexico, very far: the correlations between the two countries ran from point eight eight to point nine seven across the color measures. But those are two countries in the Americas, and the music was Western classical. The authors write that it's unclear for music built on other scales. And the essay we read adds that color symbols differ: white for purity in much of the West, mourning in many Eastern cultures. So take it as strong, not as a law.

*Visual:* two paired swatches, WHITE and GREEN, each with two small captions for two contexts, held together 3 s, beside a map-free two-row table: "US ↔ Mexico: r = .88 red–green, .96 yellow–blue, .97 light–dark, .95 saturation"; row two "nonwestern scales: untested".
*Source on screen:* "Palmer et al. 2013 (cross-culture correlations; 'unclear' for nonwestern music). Symbolism: from an essay we read."

### The editor's dial

**B08 · THE EDITOR'S DIAL · ~30 s** (tone: matter-of-fact)
> Now put that to work. The essay we read describes how editors set a rhythm of arousal with scene length: long calm scenes, short busy ones. Its rule of thumb is a calm scene of seven to twelve seconds, set against a busy one of one to three. Look at those two strips. Same length of film, very different pulse. It pairs the calm strip with cool colors and the busy one with warm.

*Visual:* two horizontal strips of equal total length. Strip one: four long blocks of 7–12 s, soft blue-grey. Strip two: about fourteen short blocks of 1–3 s, warm yellow. A playhead sweeps both with a tick at each cut. Held together 3 s.
*Source on screen:* "A rule of thumb from an essay we read: calm scenes 7–12 s, busy scenes 1–3 s."

**B09 · WHAT PACING TESTS MEASURE · ~34 s** (tone: careful)
> Pacing has been tested too, on its own. In twenty eleven, Angeline Lillard and Jennifer Peterson had sixty four-year-olds watch a fast cartoon, watch a slower one, or draw, for nine minutes. Right afterwards, the fast group did worse on four tasks of self-control and planning. That test was about pacing, not color, it measured the immediate effect, and it was one group of four-year-olds.

*Visual:* three columns (FAST CARTOON, SLOWER CARTOON, DRAW), 20 dots each; the fast column's four task bars sit lower. Frame label: "pacing, not color / immediate / four-year-olds". Held 3 s.
*Source on screen:* "Lillard & Peterson, *Pediatrics* 128(4), 2011. Bar heights are illustrative; the paper reports significant differences, not these values."

### The task

**B10 · YOUR TURN · ~44 s** (tone: handing over; **the method makes a prediction the paper can check**)
> Here's a case where two things pull against each other. Fast music pulls toward yellow, and minor pulls toward blue. The paper played every combination, so fast and minor is in the data. Use the strip: fast is strong, minor is sad, so where does fast and minor land? Then answer two more questions about it. Is its color lighter or darker than slow minor's? Is it more or less saturated than fast major's? Pause here and write down both answers.

*Visual:* the strip enlarged; two arrows from the middle: "FAST → strong, lively" and "MINOR → sad", pointing different ways. A 3 × 2 grid again with the FAST-MINOR cell highlighted and the other five cells greyed. A 6 s hold with a countdown ring and two answer slots: "LIGHTER / DARKER than slow-minor" and "MORE / LESS saturated than fast-major". Then the four-step card: "1 place tempo's push · 2 place mode's push · 3 predict: between, or closer to one pole · 4 check against the data".
*Source on screen:* "Design: Palmer et al. 2013 crossed tempo × mode (slow/medium/fast × major/minor)."

**B11 · WHAT THE FIGURE SHOWS · ~30 s** (tone: honest, plain)
> Here's what the paper's chart shows, read off its figure for the United States listeners. Fast, minor music drew colors lighter, more saturated and yellower than slow minor, and less than fast major. Tempo and mode each moved the colors on their own, and fast minor landed between. If you guessed between, the method held. If you guessed one pole, that's where it needs more than two scales.

*Visual:* the three points per dimension (saturation, light–dark, yellow–blue) as dots on three short axes, slow-minor at the low end, fast-major at the high end, fast-minor between, redrawn from Figure 2 and marked "approximate, read from chart". The user's two answer slots are ticked "between". Held 3 s together with the source.
*Source on screen:* "Palmer et al. 2013, Fig. 2 (US participants, averaged over composers); values read from the chart, approximate. Paper: faster tempo and major mode each shifted colors (P < 0.001)."
