**S01 · THE QUESTION** (~10 s, 24 words)

Two skies, one yellow and one blue-grey. Two bits of music, one quick and bright, one slow and low. Which sky goes with which?

**S02 · MOST PEOPLE AGREE** (~9 s, 21 words)

I'm Tanmay Kulkarni, for Humanitarians AI. If you put yellow on the quick one, you chose the way most listeners do.

**S03 · THE STUDY** (~16 s, 43 words)

Researchers at Berkeley tested this with real music by Bach, Mozart and Brahms. About ninety seven people, in the United States and in Mexico, picked colors for each piece. Quick, major music drew lighter, yellower colors. Slow, minor music drew darker, bluer ones.

**S04 · THE GO-BETWEEN** (~15 s, 37 words)

But a sky doesn't sound like anything, so they must be matching feeling. Listeners rated the music, and the colors, as happy or sad, strong or weak, and the two sets of ratings lined up very closely.

**S05 · THEIR OWN EXAMPLE** (~12 s, 28 words)

The paper gives its own example. Bach's fast dance feels happy and energetic, and fits a saturated yellow. Brahms's slow Adagio feels sad, and fits a dark grey.

**S06 · THE SWAP** (~10 s, 20 words)

Now swap them, and a quick tune under a blue-grey sky feels wrong, because the two feelings no longer agree.

**S07 · THE LIMIT** (~8 s, 19 words)

One limit is that this is a correlation, and the test used only two countries and Western classical music.

**S08 · TRY IT** (~12 s, 27 words)

To test it yourself, try the quick, minor case. It's in the full film: "Color Never Hears the Music: Why a Fast Tune Gets a Yellow Sky".
