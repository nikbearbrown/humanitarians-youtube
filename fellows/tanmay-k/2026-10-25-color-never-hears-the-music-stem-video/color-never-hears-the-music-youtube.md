Color Never Hears the Music: Why a Fast Tune Gets a Yellow Sky

Play quick, bright music and slow, low music, then ask which one gets the yellow sky and which gets the blue-grey one. Most listeners agree, in two different countries. This video asks what they are actually matching, because a sky doesn't sound like anything. The answer in the research is feeling: people rate music and colors on the same emotional scales, and those ratings line up very closely. You get a two-scale method to try yourself, and a case where it could be wrong: fast music in a minor key, where tempo pulls one way and mode pulls the other. Pause in the middle and make your own prediction before the paper's chart answers it.

Sources, in order of appearance:

- Angeline S. Lillard and Jennifer Peterson (2011), "The immediate impact of different types of television on young children's executive function", Pediatrics 128(4), 644-649

What this video claims, and what it does not:

- The study: 48 listeners in the United States and 49 in Mexico heard 18 fifty-second orchestral selections by Bach, Mozart and Brahms (slow, medium and fast, in major and minor keys) and picked, from 37 colors, the five that fit best and the five that fit worst. In both countries, faster tempo and major mode each moved the choices toward lighter, more saturated, yellower colors; slower tempo and minor mode moved them toward darker, greyer, bluer ones.
- The two scales on the strip (happy to sad, strong to weak) are the two emotion dimensions the paper found for both the music and the colors. The correlations between the emotion ratings of the music and of the colors chosen for it, from 0.89 to 0.99, are the paper's. For the US listeners they were 0.97 (happy/sad), 0.99 (lively/dreary), 0.96 (strong/weak) and 0.89 (angry/calm).
- It is a correlation. The authors say it does not establish that emotion causes the match, and that a direct link between color and sound is not ruled out.
- It was tested in two countries, with Western classical music. The authors write that it is unclear for music built on other scales.
- "Rate the sound, rate the color, see whether they agree" is our teaching method for using the idea. It is not the paper's procedure.
- The Bach and Brahms examples are the paper's own illustration of its hypothesis, not measured picks.
- The paper crossed tempo and mode fully, so fast minor music is in its data. The three positions shown for it are approximate, read off the paper's Figure 2 (US listeners, averaged over composers), and are labelled that way on screen.
- The pacing study measured one thing: 60 four-year-olds watched a fast-paced cartoon, an educational cartoon, or drew, for 9 minutes, and the fast-paced group did worse on four tasks of executive function right afterwards. That is pacing, not color, it is the immediate effect, and it is one group of children. The bars and dots on screen are illustrative.
- Three details come from the essay we read, and we describe them as the essay's rather than as findings: that babies sort colors into categories by about six months and name them by about eighteen, that color symbols differ across regions, and a rule of thumb for calm and busy scene lengths in editing.
- The skies, swatches, tunes and diagrams are illustrations made for this video. They are not data.
