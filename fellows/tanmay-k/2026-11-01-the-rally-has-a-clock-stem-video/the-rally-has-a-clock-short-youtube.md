In my tiny Pong, a perfect defender loses on the sixth shot. Why? #Shorts

In a tiny Pong I built, a perfect defender loses on the sixth shot, and nobody makes a mistake. An essay says Pong has no perfect defense; this Short shows when that is true. Two bars explain it: how far the paddle can travel while the ball crosses, and how far the worst shot makes it go (about half the field). The ball speeds up 5% every shot, so the first bar sinks, and on the sixth shot it drops below the line by 8.4 pixels. The arithmetic says shot six, and a search that tries every aim agrees.

Full film: The Rally Has a Clock: When Is "No Perfect Defense" True in Pong?

What this Short claims, and what it does not:
- It is our own simulation of a simplified Pong (field 858 by 525, paddle 60, ball starting at 6 per tick, speeding up 5% every shot, paddle speed 2.0). No spin, no human reaction time, no real machine.
- The defender is assumed to see the ball's direction the instant it is hit and to wait in the middle; the attacker can aim anywhere.
- "No perfect defense" is true here only because the ball speeds up: with a ball that never speeds up, paddles at speeds 2.0 and 3.0 held for all 200 shots tested.
- Two phrases come from an essay by Seth Brown (Zebonastic, Humanitarians AI Fellow), "Pong: Why the Simplest Game Is the Hardest to Explain".
