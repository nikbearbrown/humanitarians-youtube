Building the Answer Key, Grader and Baseline — AI Debt Tracker, week 2

The agent that tracks the bonds and loans financing the AI build-out still does not exist. This
week built the things that will judge it: the answer key it is graded against, the grader that
scores it, and the do-nothing baseline it has to beat. Then it checked the grader itself against
debt tables printed in real reports — and the grader lost.

THE ANSWER KEY. Every quarterly and annual report from January 2024 to September 2026, for 100
companies — the 25 AI-sector companies and 75 others to compare against. 1,093 reports, 9,653
bonds. 900 of those reports list their debt bond by bond, 41 only by deal, and 152 only as
totals, so each quarter is graded at the detail its own report actually gives. The agent never
sees any of it.

THE GRADER. A debt list is scored three ways. Bond by bond, an entry counts as matched only if
the currency, the interest rate (to 0.01 of a point) and the due year all agree and the amount
is within 1% — and each true bond can be claimed by at most one answer, so one correct guess
cannot be counted against several. Then deal by deal, then totals. Every score carries a 95%
bootstrap interval, so a small difference between two runs is never read as an improvement.

BUT WHO CHECKS THE CHECKER? I compared the answer key against the debt tables printed in ten
real 10-Qs and 10-Ks. The first pass agreed on eight. Every single disagreement was a bug in my
grader, not an error in the report:

  1. Repaid notes were still counted as owed (Oracle)
  2. Two bonds collapsed into one entry (AMD)
  3. Debt acquired in a purchase dropped entirely (Broadcom)
  4. Due dates read as issue dates
  5. A currency that the filing never states (Equinix)

After fixing all five: 10 of 10.

AND 10 OF 10 IS A CALIBRATION, NOT A SCORE. Those ten reports are the ones that found the five
bugs, so the grader cannot help but agree with them afterwards. For the honest check I picked
three untouched reports, read them once, and graded them once. Two of three agree. The one miss
is that company's own tagging errors — recorded for next week rather than tuned away. Three
reports is a small check and it is the only number here that was not read while the bugs were
being fixed.

THE FLOOR, AND WHAT IT HIDES. The simplest possible tracker copies last quarter's debt forward.
It caught none of the 36 new bonds. Zero. It still finds 90.7% of all bonds (87.5–93.5%), and
it reproduces 18 of 47 quarters exactly — and the 90.7% is high for exactly the same reason the
zero is zero: a quarter's debt mostly does not move. The agent is not being asked to beat
90.7%. It is being asked to find what changed.

THE HEADROOM. 30 of the 32 new bonds that could be searched for at all were announced in a
filing published in time to be read — four of the 36 carry no tagged interest rate, so there is
nothing to search for. Of 16 bonds that disappeared, 9 simply reached their due date and one had
no rate to search for; of the remaining 6 early repayments, 5 were announced. The distance
between doing nothing and reading the filings is almost the whole gap. That distance is the job.

ONE SURPRISE. EDGAR quietly moved 43 of 495 filing timestamps by four to five hours between two
reads of the same filings. The shift is 4 hours on summer dates and 5 in winter, which matches
US Eastern to UTC — most likely a time-zone change, though EDGAR published no note, so that is
an inference rather than a fact. Either way the lesson holds: the server that stops the agent
seeing the future now anchors each cut-off to the report itself, not to a clock.

NEXT. Fix that one company's data problems, check a fresh set of reports, and start the agent's
document reader — the part that turns a filing into sentences it can point to, so it never has
to type a number itself.

Every figure on screen is a prop injected from figdata.json at build time, not typed by hand.
The build asserts the key relationships and fails rather than let a number drift — including
that the three grading levels sum to all 1,093 reports, that the held-out hand-check score stays
BELOW the calibrated one (otherwise the distinction this video is built on would be empty), that
the baseline's recall stays high while its new-bond count stays at zero, and that 36 − 4 = 32.
Three claims in the video are not measured in figdata.json: that the agent is unbuilt, that the
timestamp shift is an inference, and what the one held-out miss was. Each is attributed on
screen. See FACTCHECK.md in the project folder for what still needs confirmation before
publishing.
