Measuring How Private Marks Move and Propagate — Private AI Valuation Agent, week 8

For seven weeks I was building a panel of per-share prices for private AI companies out of SEC
filings. This week I stopped building it and started measuring it. 5,479 marks, four questions,
and all four came back against expectation.

HOW OFTEN DOES A PRICE ACTUALLY CHANGE? The plan expected 30–40% of consecutive observations to
be carried forward unchanged. Measured: 25.0%. And that is not an artifact of where I drew the
line — widening "unchanged" to anything under 0.1% gives 26.3%, and to anything under 1% gives
29.6%. Even the most generous reading stays under the plan's band. Private marks move more than
the plan assumed.

IS THAT RATE THE SAME EVERYWHERE? No, and this is the part that mattered most. Split by company,
the same statistic runs from 0.0% for Groq to 48.5% for X.AI. Anthropic and OpenAI are repriced
at nearly every observation; X.AI and SpaceX are carried forward a third to a half of the time.
One number across the whole panel would have been true and useless.

DO TWO MANAGERS PRICING THE SAME COMPANY ON THE SAME DATE AGREE? Mostly not. Across 130
same-date groups the median spread between the highest and lowest price is 10.8%, and 92 of
those groups hold more than one distinct price level.

But that is not automatically disagreement about value — some of it is a new round that some
managers have booked and others have not. So I opened one window: Anthropic preferred stock, 8
managers, 11 marks, across two consecutive period ends, running from $140.97 to $261.57. You can
see the old level, you can see the new one, and you can see two managers sitting between them,
mid-crossing. That is a round arriving, one manager at a time.

HOW FAST DOES A NEW LEVEL TRAVEL? I took every price level that three or more managers adopted —
37 of them — and measured how long it took to reach half its holders. Median 30 days. 15 of the
37 get there on the very same period end. The slowest takes 92.

Two things that number is not. It cannot be shorter than the reporting calendar: across 55
period ends the panel sees only 5 distinct days of the month, so this measures observability,
not diligence. And the figure originally claimed the biggest events travel fastest — I checked,
and it does not hold. 27 days against 30 is a three-day gap on nine events, and the smaller
events reach the same period end more often, 12 of 28 against 3 of 9. The caption came out.

Every one of those numbers runs behind a guard: 294 unpriced marks and 301 on a split-blocked
series are excluded before any statistic is computed, and no statistic runs over an
unadjudicated split or an incomplete run.

And what none of this shows: these are fund marks, not transactions. Nobody traded at these
prices. This project publishes what funds wrote down, and nothing more.
