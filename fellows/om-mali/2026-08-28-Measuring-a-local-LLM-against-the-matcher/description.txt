Measuring a Local LLM Against the Matcher — Private AI Valuation Agent, week 5

I tried to replace my rule-based entity matcher with an 8B language model running locally on
my own machine. It lost. This is the measurement.

The model got exactly what the rules get: the name on the filing, the security title, the fund
that filed it, and a list of 11 candidate companies. No price, no answer. 322 calls against the
same 322 labelled names I built the week before. Zero failures.

Precision went from 0.9959 to 0.9449. Five points sounds survivable until you count it in
holdings instead of names: one wrong record becomes 196.

And it failed the same way every time — it promoted resemblances. It saw a company called
Hyperscale Data and answered Scale AI, because, it said, Hyperscale Data is Scale AI's parent
company. That is not true; it invented a corporate fact. It saw a term loan to Scaled Agile and
said Scale AI again, at full confidence, across 32 holdings. And it saw an internal Fidelity
security code and called it X.AI on three matching characters.

The finding that actually changes the plan is the confidence number. The model reported full
confidence on 315 of its 322 answers — including 12 of the 15 it got wrong. So next week's
human review queue cannot be sorted by how sure the model is. That was the plan.

One thing did work. Every mistake was the model ADDING a company; the one time it helped, it
took one away. So a version allowed only to veto scores a perfect 1.0000 — on four rows. Four.
I built it, measured it, and left it switched off. It is a decent sceptic and a poor proposer.

The plan committed in advance to what happens if there is no lift: keep the rules, and say so.
So I am saying so.

Scope, honestly: one model, one size, one quantization (llama3.1:8b, Q4_K_M, temperature 0,
seed 7). A larger model might clear the bar. None was tried, and that is an open question — the
claim here is that this 8B model failed at this task, not that AI cannot do it.

Next week: the human review queue.
