Building the Human Review Queue — Private AI Valuation Agent, week 6

This week I built the part of the pipeline that knows when to stop and ask a person.

The project turns SEC filings into share prices for private companies. The hard part was never
the arithmetic — it is working out which company a filing is actually talking about, when the
same company arrives under dozens of different spellings.

5,806 holdings. The matcher resolved 4,537 of them on its own, off a registered identifier or a
name it already knew. On the other 1,269 it refused to guess. Those stopped and waited for me.
Nothing was dropped for being difficult.

Three things made the queue workable:

ONE ANSWER, NOT TWENTY-FOUR. What stopped was 42 cards, but only 8 actual questions. X.AI
alone arrives under 24 different spellings — some sharing an issuer name and differing only in
the security title. The answer is recorded against the company, not the spelling, so one answer
clears all 24 and spelling number 25 never reaches a person.

A PAUSED QUESTION SURVIVES THE PROCESS. When the graph stops it writes its whole state to
Postgres. I tested that by pausing in one process and answering in a completely separate one.
Then I got a second test I did not ask for: the database server crashed, and when it came back
all 42 questions were still sitting there. That is one accident, not a durability guarantee —
but it is the test I would not have thought to design.

IT CAUGHT A SPLIT THAT LOOKED LIKE A CRASH. Perplexity's share count went from 6,081 to 60,810
while the filed value stayed at $4,228,993.75 — identical to the cent. That is a 10-for-1 stock
split, and priced naively it would have entered my price history as a 90% crash. Two other
price steps looked exactly the same and were not splits at all: SpaceX's is the same fund
reporting common stock at $81 and preferred at $810 on the same day, and Anthropic's is an
ordinary funding round. Same shape, three different causes — a detector that treated them alike
would be wrong two times out of three.

The thing I most want to be clear about: the software decided nothing. It routed, it grouped,
and it presented. All 45 recorded decisions carry a human name and a written reason, and the
code rejects a decision missing either. Seventy-eight percent resolved unaided and twenty-two
percent needed a person — that is the design working, not a shortfall.

Every decision is now also a regression test, so a future change to the matcher cannot quietly
overturn a judgment already made.
