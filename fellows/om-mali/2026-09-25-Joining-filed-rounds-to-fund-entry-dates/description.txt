Joining Filed Rounds to Fund Entry Dates — Private AI Valuation Agent, week 9

For eight weeks this project ran on one SEC form. N-PORT tells you what a position is worth at a
month end. It never tells you when it was bought. This week I added two filings that do, and the
whole episode is about what the join between them runs on.

Form D is filed by the COMPANY when it sells shares, and gives offering dates and amounts. The
restricted securities footnote required by Reg S-X 12-12 is filed by the FUND in its annual
report, and gives acquisition dates and cost. Two different filers, describing the same shares.

WHY THE OBVIOUS JOIN IS WRONG. The natural way to find a company's Form D filings is to search
for its name. I did that across 49 quarters and got 706 rows. 595 of them — 84.3% — are not the
company. They are feeder funds named after it, raising money to buy its shares on the secondary
market. "Anthropic Jan 2026 a Series of CGF2021 LLC" is one of them, and the amount it reports
selling is its own raise, not Anthropic's. Add those up, call it a funding round, and you have
published numbers no company ever filed.

AND NO BETTER STRING MATCHING FIXES IT. One filer from 2014 normalises to exactly the same
string as X.AI and is a completely different company. That is not a normalisation bug to be
tuned away; it is two real entities with the same name. So the join runs on an EDGAR identifier
a person has confirmed. The code proposes. A human decides.

FIVE LAYOUTS OF THE SAME FOOTNOTE. The second source has no bulk dataset and no required format.
The regulation says what must be disclosed, not what the columns are called, so every fund family
writes it differently — four use tables with different headings, one uses no table at all. That
got me 249 positions from 31 filings, 227 of them with a per-position cost. The other 22 are one
filer's, reported at fund level rather than per position, and the build asserts that exactly one
filer does that.

WHAT THE JOIN BOUGHT. Two things the panel could not say before. Entry dates reach back to
20 January 2015, 7.9 years before the panel's first mark for that company. But only three of the
seven companies genuinely reach back — for the rest I have only the latest annual and
semi-annual reports, so this is a floor on the record, not a history. And an exposure map: 90
manager–company pairs across 30 managers and 10 companies, using each filer's own reported
percentage of net assets. That is concentration, not conviction. A large percentage is a fact
about the fund's size as much as about the position.

THE PART I DID NOT EXPECT. With both sources joined, 10 of 18 fund acquisition dates fall on the
exact day a company reported selling shares. The clearest case is Databricks, 22 October 2019 —
five separate fund managers independently name that same day. The company files because it sold.
The funds file because they own. Neither filing cites the other, which is what makes the
agreement evidence rather than arithmetic. It is not proof of causation: buying on the day an
issuer reports a first sale is also consistent with a secondary purchase that settled that day.

AND THE DENOMINATOR IS DOING THE WORK. That 56% counts only purchases made while a company was
still filing Form D. One company stopped in mid-2022, so its later buys have no round to be near
and the distance to the nearest one measures the end of the archive rather than a fund's
behaviour. Counted against every acquisition date instead, 56% reads as 33%. Both readings are
in the video, at the same size.

What none of this shows: no valuation, because neither source carries shares outstanding. No
return, because a footnote cost over a later mark is not one when the share count may have
changed in between. No causation.
