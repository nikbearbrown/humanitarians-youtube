One Sign-In, Many Books — Medhavi Hub

A student signs in once and can open every textbook they're entitled to. But
every textbook is a separate site, at its own address, on its own server — and
none of them has your password. So how does a website that has never seen you
know who you are, and know you're allowed in?

It gets handed a ticket. This is what's in the ticket, why the site that holds
it can't check it, and the one genuinely clever part: how logging out can kill
every outstanding ticket everywhere without keeping a list of cancelled ones.

WHAT YOU WILL KNOW AFTER WATCHING
What goes in the ticket — who you are, which one book, and when it expires.
Why it's signed, and why holding a ticket and checking one are different powers.
Why the receiving site has to call home before letting anyone in. Why logging
out, by itself, changes nothing when tickets are already out there with hours
left to run. Why the obvious fix — a list of cancelled tickets — trades one hard
problem for a permanent one. And the alternative: write down one timestamp, and
every ticket older than it simply stops counting.

A NOTE ON ACCURACY
Every claim here was checked against the medhavi-hub source after filming. The
ticket contents, the 24-hour expiry, the access check before the ticket is
issued, the call back to the hub, and the logout-timestamp mechanism — including
the complete absence of any revocation list — all verified.

One correction, stated plainly because it matters: the video says that changing
one character makes the signature stop matching. In the code as it stands, a
failed signature check is caught and the ticket is then read as unsigned data,
so tampering does not reject it. Other checks still apply — the user must exist,
be unbanned, hold real entitlement, and the ticket must post-date their last
logout — but the signature itself is not currently the gate this video describes
it as. The fix is a one-line change in the hub. The full verification trail,
including a second related finding, is published with the source package.

The design reasoning in the video is sound. The claim about what the
implementation enforces was too generous, and that is worth saying out loud
rather than quietly.
