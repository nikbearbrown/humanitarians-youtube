# STEM Video: How AI Agents Are Changing Payments — Narration Draft

## B00A — Presenter intro
Hi, I'm Aishwarya from the Mycroft team.
This video is about AI agents in payments: what changes when an agent stops answering questions about your money and starts moving it.

## B00 — Cold open
In June 2026, an AI agent in Finland bought a coffee tasting package and paid for it with a real bank card. A person gave the instructions. The agent made the purchase and the payment. It was a small order, but a new kind of transaction.

## B01 — Where most people meet agents: customer service
Method: start with the agent most people have already met. In February 2024, Klarna said its AI assistant, built on OpenAI technology, had 2.3 million conversations in its first month: two-thirds of its customer service chats, the equivalent work of 700 full-time agents. Those are Klarna's own numbers. And in 2025, its CEO said the company was hiring human agents again, because cutting costs had driven too much of the decision, and quality had suffered.

## B02 — Read access versus write access
Klarna's assistant handled refunds, returns and payment questions. Its announcement doesn't say whether it could move money on its own. That's the line that matters. An agent that can only read your account can give you a wrong answer. An agent with write access, one that can pay an invoice or make a purchase, can make a wrong payment. The risk changes from a bad reply to a bad transaction.

## B03 — The first live agentic payments
That line was crossed in live tests this June. On June 2, ING in the Netherlands used Mastercard Agent Pay to buy concert tickets, with the customer approving the final choice. Days later, Nordea and Mastercard completed Finland's first live agentic payment: the agent handled the purchase and the payment end to end, from the customer's instructions. Agentic tokens kept the real card details away from the agent. Both were pilots, not services customers can use yet.

## B04 — The open question: accountability
So who is responsible when an agent makes a wrong payment? Google's Agent Payments Protocol, AP2, tries to make that answerable. Your request is signed as an intent mandate. Your approval of the exact items and price is signed as a cart mandate. Together they form what Google calls a non-repudiable audit trail: proof of what you asked for and what you approved. But proof isn't liability. Google's announcement doesn't say who pays when something goes wrong. That question is still open.

## B05 — Handoff
Your turn. Pick one payment you'd let an agent make for you, and write down its limits: the most it could spend, and what it must ask you before it pays. You've just written an intent mandate by hand.
