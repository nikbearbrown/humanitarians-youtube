# Frictional log — Matter Waves (QM Vol. 1, Ch. 2)

## 2026-09-02 — the second build, and what repeating the skill exposed

> Written 2026-09-28, after the week it describes. Reconstructed from
> [beat_sheet.json](beat_sheet.json) and the Week 14 report; not a contemporaneous entry.

- **Beat sheet:** [beat_sheet.json](beat_sheet.json) — 14 beats, 233.5s
- **Sibling build:** [Chapter 1](../2026-09-02-claude-sri-why-classical-physics-failed/), same day

**What I was working on.** Chapter 2 through the same skill, on the same day as
Chapter 1. Two builds back to back was the point — one build tells you the skill
runs, two tell you whether it generalises.

**What I tried, and what I expected.** I expected Chapter 2 to be easier because
the machinery was warm. I expected the structure to differ from Chapter 1, because
a chapter about a single idea (matter waves) is not shaped like a chapter about two
historical failures.

**Where it resisted, and what I did next.**

- **The structure came out nearly identical, and that worried me.** Both chapters
  landed on 14 beats, two acts, the same bookend positions, within six seconds of
  the same runtime. My first reaction was that the skill was imposing its template
  rather than reading the chapter. I went back through Chapter 2's acts to check
  whether the split was real — "The Wave Nobody Ordered" (the hypothesis and its
  experimental confirmation) and "One Electron at a Time" (what a single particle
  does) are genuinely two different claims, not one claim cut in half. I kept it.
  But I do not think two samples is enough to know the template isn't driving.
- **The cold open had to carry a fact, not just a mood.** The register demands a
  historical hook. "De Broglie proposed matter waves" is not a hook. The committee
  almost rejecting the thesis is — it makes the idea's strangeness concrete before
  any equation appears. Finding that took longer than writing the derivation beats.
- **The scale beat was the hardest to make honest.** B09 puts the electron, a
  buckyball and larger objects on a logarithmic scale bar. A linear axis makes the
  comparison unreadable; a log axis makes it readable but can flatter the claim.
  I kept the log scale and labelled it as such rather than picking the axis that
  made the point look biggest.
- **ψ(x) → |ψ(x)|² needed to be an animation, not a caption.** B10 is the beat
  where the chapter stops being about diffraction and starts being about
  probability. Writing the modulus-squared as text would have made it a notational
  step. Animating the transformation makes it the conceptual turn it actually is.

**What Claude contributed, and what I accepted, changed or rejected.**

- Accepted: the two-act split, after checking it against the chapter myself rather
  than taking it.
- Accepted: the biprism framing for B07 — it isolates "one path split in two"
  more cleanly than a double slit does.
- Rejected: a linear scale on B09.
- Rejected: opening on the equation. λ=h/p is B02, not B00.

**What I understand now, and what I still do not.**

I understand that running a skill twice in one day is a cheaper test of the skill
than running it once carefully — the second build is where you find out what the
skill is deciding *for* you.

I still do not know whether the near-identical structure across both chapters is
the chapters agreeing or the template asserting itself. Two builds cannot tell me.
A chapter with a genuinely different shape — one long mechanism rather than two
episodes — would settle it, and I have not built one.

I also do not know whether the Bragg golden-test figure in B04 is correct. It is
shown being derived, which the register requires, but no factcheck pass was run
against the chapter source.
