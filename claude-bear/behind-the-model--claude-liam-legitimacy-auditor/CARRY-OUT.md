# CARRY-OUT — GATE C

Written before any other narration. Every beat exists to make this sentence survivable.

## The line

> **An AI answer feels legitimate when it's fluent. It only IS legitimate
> when someone is named to answer for it.**

(Compressed from the source's B07 summary beat — "Context changes the
legitimacy structure of the same output... An AI output can pass all three or
fail all three depending entirely on context, and the audit makes that
explicit" — restated in Plain register around the source's own bedside case.
It states mechanism, not a design judgment, so nothing needed removing.)

## The wrong guess it defeats

That a confident, fluent AI answer is already trustworthy — it reads like it
knows what it's talking about, so it must be safe to act on. That's the
cognitive question only: does it *feel* taken for granted, natural? It says
nothing about the moral question — is a specific person or role named as
accountable if the answer is wrong. The same sentence, word for word, can
hold in a finance committee (the CFO owns the number) and fail at a bedside
(no one is named to own it), because the rooms differ in who answers for it,
not in how the sentence reads.

## The secondhand test

*If someone repeats only this sentence in a meeting next week, is it still true?*

Yes — it compresses the one distinction that matters (feeling right vs.
someone answering for it) without claiming fluent answers are always wrong or
that every context needs the identical accountable party.

## What it deliberately does not say

- Not a claim that fluent AI answers are usually wrong — it's a claim about
  what fluency can and can't prove (direction A, B05).
- Not a claim that disclosure or hedging fixes the gap — a labeled or
  clearly-synthetic answer still needs a named accountable party; a
  disclaimer isn't a mechanism (direction B, B06).
- Not a claim that one fix works everywhere — the accountable party and the
  review step have to be named per context, not assumed from a template.
