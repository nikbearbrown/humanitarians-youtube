# CARRY-OUT — GATE C

Written before any other narration. Every beat exists to make this
sentence survivable.

## The line

> **Ask Claude to escalate a customer issue, and it doesn't forward
> anything — it builds the full case, picks the target by fixed tier
> rules, and waits for you to say go.**

## The wrong guess it defeats

That "escalate this" works like flagging a message — one click, sent to
whoever seems right. It doesn't. The skill runs a checklist first
(what's broken, who's affected, how long, what's been tried), checks
that against its own "handle in support vs. escalate" criteria, gathers
context, and quantifies impact across five dimensions before it even
decides who the brief goes to — and the tier that gets picked (support,
engineering, product, security, or leadership) follows fixed rules, not
a guess about who seems right.

## The secondhand test

*If someone repeats only this sentence in a meeting next week, is it
still true?*

Yes — it compresses the one distinction that matters (a checklist and a
confirm gate, not a one-click forward) without needing a second sentence.

## What it deliberately does not say

- **Not a verdict on whether the checklist is the right amount of
  friction.** The source's B03/BVDT framed "what it gets right" / "what
  it bites" as Teardown judgment. Plain keeps the same underlying
  mechanism (nothing sent until the brief is built and confirmed) as a
  fact about sequencing, not a critique of the design choice.
- **Not that every issue gets escalated.** The same checklist can
  conclude "handle in support" — a documented workaround, a
  configuration fix, or a previously-resolved pattern never reaches
  engineering, product, security, or leadership at all.
- **Not a real Acme Corp support ticket.** "API returning 500 errors
  intermittently for Acme Corp" is the skill's own documented usage
  example, used here to make the checklist and the tier choice
  concrete — not a claim about an actual customer or incident.
