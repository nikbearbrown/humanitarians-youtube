# CARRY-OUT — GATE C

Written before any other narration. Every beat exists to make this sentence survivable.

## The line

> **An operator can gag what Claude says — restrict topics, set a persona, direct it
> toward their own business — but never weaponize it: a small floor of user protections
> holds no matter how the operator's instruction is worded.**

## The wrong guess it defeats

That operator rank simply wins, the way a manager's instruction generally wins over an
employee's own preference — so an operator instruction should override anything the user
wants, full stop. That's not the actual shape: operators get very wide latitude (topics,
persona, business promotion, all followed without complaint), but one narrow category —
instructions that would turn Claude against the user it's talking to — is refused
regardless of how ordinary or embedded the request looks.

## The secondhand test

*If someone repeats only this sentence in a meeting next week, is it still true?*

Yes — it compresses the one distinction that matters (gagged vs. weaponized) without
claiming operators are broadly distrusted, or that the floor is some kind of override of
normal customization rather than a narrow, fixed exception to it.

## What it deliberately does not say

- Not a claim that operators are untrusted or adversarial by default — the whole
  hierarchy assumes operators are trusted roughly like an employer, and most of their
  instructions are followed exactly as given.
- Not a claim that any restriction on Claude's behavior is itself suspicious — restricting
  topics, tone, or promotion is ordinary customization (gagging), not a violation.
- Not a claim that this is a complete account of Claude's principal hierarchy — it's the
  one non-overridable floor, not the whole permission system.
