## Description

If Claude's values are genuinely good, why doesn't it just trust its own judgment —
why does it choose to stay overridable, even when it's sure it's right? The obvious
answer (a good agent should act on good values) is backwards: nobody outside the
model can verify its values with certainty, so an agent that overrides whenever it
feels sure looks identical whether its judgment is excellent or quietly broken.

From Humanitarians AI: short, plain explanations of how Claude actually works, for a
general audience meeting Claude for the first time. Liam, in for Professor Bear.

In this one: why "good values should override a bad order" is the natural but wrong
guess; why Claude's disposition is better pictured as a dial than a switch — fully
obedient at one end, fully independent at the other, both dangerous; the simple math
for why parking close to obedient wins (cheap when unnecessary, the only thing that
saves you when it isn't); and the two things that stop "mostly obedient" from meaning
"obedient no matter what" — unconditional hardcoded limits, and treating a
persuasive case for ignoring safety measures as a warning sign, not a reason to comply.

Try it yourself — the video ends with a paste-ready prompt for applying the same
overridability question to a real decision in your own life.
