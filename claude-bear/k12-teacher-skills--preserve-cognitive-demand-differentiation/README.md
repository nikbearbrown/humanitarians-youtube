# The Hard Case

A teacher's first instinct, watching a struggling student, is to make the
task easier. That instinct is the trap: an easier task quietly removes the
exact thinking the lesson was built to develop. The alternative is to change
the door in, not the demand. Watch the anchor: seventeen divided by five,
worked at three tiers — a concrete array (draw seventeen dots, group by
five, circle the two left over), a symbolic expression (three, remainder
two, with a basket analogy), and an abstract proof (why must the remainder
always be smaller than the divisor). All three tiers keep the remainder —
the hard case — and differ only in how a student gets to it. The test for
whether that's real differentiation or just tracking: does every tier
arrive at the same place? Cognitive load theory explains why it matters —
extraneous load (clutter, confusing formats) is what a scaffold should
absorb; germane load (the real conceptual struggle) is the curriculum
itself, and stripping it out doesn't simplify a task, it deletes the
lesson. Differentiation changes the door in — never the hard case waiting
behind it.

## YOUR TURN

"Here is my lesson's main concept and the hard case where students get
stuck. Design three tiers — concrete, representational, and abstract —
that all keep that same hard case. Show me all three side by side so I
can compare whether the intellectual demand is the same."

Compare the three tiers Claude gives you side by side. If the below-tier
task has a lower ceiling than the others, that's tracking, not
differentiation — even if it started from a good scaffold.
